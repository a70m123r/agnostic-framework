From squeeze to pull

Marriage as a kernel canon under pressure — what it was solving, where it's going dormant, and how its function is being absorbed by other carriers in different regions. With regional heat map, three speculative bubbles, a four-carrier analysis of the open action-space dormancy opens, and the framework's v1.2 refactor: canon doesn't dissolve, it sleeps, and if it returns it returns refactored with whatever the dormancy imparted.

01The undercurrent in any union

The framework's symbiosis-as-pushout primitive describes what happens when two observers with complementary cone-profiles merge into a new observer that neither was alone. Mitochondria and the ancestral host cell. Printing press and literacy. Internet and human attention. Each of these is told, in retrospect, as a story of successful union. None of them was, while it was happening, free of tension. The framework is precise about this — and v5 of the casual manifesto names it explicitly. A merger that produces a new observer leaves a residue: the friction between what each side was and what the merged thing now needs. The friction does not vanish when the merger compiles. It moves underground and becomes the substrate the merged thing now has to manage.

This essay is about one merger we usually do not call a merger: the institution of marriage. It is treated as the smallest possible union — two people, one household — and therefore as the cleanest case. The framework's reading is opposite. Marriage is one of the most heavily-engineered carrier artifacts that human canon has ever produced, and the undercurrent tension it manages is enormous. The institution is built specifically to absorb that tension and convert it into structure. When the institution dissolves, the tension does not. It has to be re-absorbed somewhere else, by some other carrier. The interesting question is not whether marriage is fading. In many places, that is empirically settled. The interesting question is: what is absorbing the function that the institution was performing? And that is a question whose answer differs dramatically across the regions of the planet — in ways that map onto religion, climate, government, ideology, and the specific squeeze that produced the institution in each place.

Before we get to the regional heat map, three biology cases worth holding in mind. They are not analogies. They are the same shape as marriage running at different substrates — and the framework's procedural-lineage view says we should expect that.

Mitochondrial ROS leak

The endosymbiotic merger that produced eukaryotic cells two billion years ago looks, in retrospect, like the cleanest case of cooperative union in biology. It is also leaky. Mitochondria still produce reactive oxygen species — a byproduct of their energy work that damages the cell that hosts them. The merger compiled. The tension persists. Most cellular aging is downstream of this unresolved residue.

Lichen contract

A lichen is a fungus and an alga (sometimes a cyanobacterium) running a long-term partnership: the fungus provides structure, the alga provides photosynthate. Recent work shows the fungus aggressively regulates how much sugar the alga can keep. The union is real. The terms are not equal. Under stress, the fungus can starve the alga before the alga can leave.

Coral bleaching

Coral and its photosynthetic algae (zooxanthellae) run a similarly load-bearing partnership. When the water gets too warm, the coral expels the algae — preserving itself in the short term, condemning itself in the long term. The merger only holds inside a narrow band of conditions. Outside the band, the dissolution looks fast, but the band itself was the canon, and the band has been shifting for millions of years.

The pattern across the three: union does not abolish tension; it sets a band inside which the tension is productive. When the band shifts — environmentally, energetically, demographically — the union does not gracefully retire. It either refactors or breaks. The framework's claim about marriage is that we are watching exactly this dynamic at the social-substrate ring of the procedural lineage, and the shape it takes in each region is determined by what the band there has been doing.

02What marriage was actually solving

The framework treats canon as a residue of pressure. A long-lived institution is what was left behind when many generations of human observers, running their own A⁺/A⁻ loops at different scales, kept compiling the same answer to a question they could not afford to leave open. To read marriage as canon, we have to ask: what question were people answering, and what would have happened if no answer had been compiled?

The honest answer is: many overlapping questions, with different ones load-bearing in different places. The institution we now collapse into one word was, originally, a stack of solutions to a stack of squeezes.

Paternity certainty in a pre-genetic-testing world. Before DNA testing, the only reliable mechanism for a father to know which children were his was to constrain who his partner could be with. Marriage as exclusive sexual contract is the canon that compiled around that constraint. The function looks barbaric from inside a world that has paternity testing. It was load-bearing for the world that did not.

Property and inheritance under scarcity. Where surviving the winter or the drought depended on hereditable resources — land, livestock, water rights, tools — the institution that decided whose children inherited what was the institution that decided whether your family line continued. Marriage as the regulator of inheritance is a different canon from marriage as paternity certainty. They overlap but they are not identical. Cultures with weak private property (some hunter-gatherer arrangements, some pastoral commons) had much looser marriage forms because the squeeze was less acute.

Child-rearing infrastructure. Human children are obligate-helpless for longer than the children of any other ape. Two committed adults — and usually more, drawn from kin networks the marriage codified — is the minimum viable team for getting a child to reproductive age in most pre-modern environments. The exact configuration varied: extended household, polygyny in some grassland economies, matrilineal kinship in others. The constant was that something had to absorb the cost of childhood, and the institution named whoever was on the hook.

Alliance and conflict reduction. Between families, between clans, between dynasties, between trading partners, marriage was the protocol for converting potential conflict into structured exchange. Royal marriages mapped this most visibly. Village-level marriages did the same work at smaller scale: a daughter from family A married into family B became a permanent reason for the two families to refrain from feuding.

Labor pooling. Pre-industrial households were production units, not just consumption units. Bread baking, cloth making, brewing, butchering, gardening, animal husbandry, child rearing, elder care — these required different skills and roughly continuous labor. A two-adult household with complementary trained skill-sets was a small firm. Marriage was the contract that constituted the firm.

Care of the old. Before state pensions and before nuclear-family geographic mobility, the people who looked after you when you could no longer look after yourself were the ones you had spent a life producing obligations toward. Marriage and the kin network it codified was the elder-care system.

Read together, these are not romantic descriptions. They are the squeezes that the institution compiled to absorb. The framework's term squeeze event usually refers to acute pressure that forces canon to refactor fast (a pandemic, a war, an asteroid). But there is a slower form — a chronic squeeze where the pressure is continuous and the canon compiles over generations. Marriage is the chronic-squeeze case at the social-substrate ring, and the canon it produced is one of the densest carriers in human history. It has been propagated through every available channel — religion, law, economy, kin, art, ritual, language itself.

The question this essay is asking — the question the heat map is going to make visible — is what happens to a chronic-squeeze canon when most of the squeezes that produced it have, in some regions, been substantially relieved by other carriers. DNA testing handles paternity. State pensions handle elder care. Day care, schools, and mass-produced goods handle the production functions. Welfare states handle inheritance for households with little to inherit. Police and law handle inter-family conflict reduction. Each of these is a separate carrier that absorbs one of the original load functions. Where many of them have arrived, the institution is no longer doing the work it was designed to do. It is still standing because canon is sticky, but the load-bearing has migrated.

03Regional renderings: a canon-dormancy heat map

The map below assigns each of fourteen world regions a Canon Dissolution Index (CDI) on a 0–10 scale. The index is a rough composite of four observable signals: (a) crude marriage rate relative to its long-run regional baseline, (b) share of first births to cohabiting (unmarried) couples, (c) legal-and-social parity between cohabitation and marriage (does the law treat them equivalently for child-rearing, property, inheritance, taxation), and (d) the median age at first marriage. The CDI does not measure whether people are partnering. It measures whether the institutional carrier is still load-bearing for the function it was compiled to absorb.

One region — East Asia — gets a different mark. There the institution dissolved fast, no single successor protocol has absorbed the function yet, and several candidate carriers are being scouted simultaneously. Marriage rates have collapsed; cohabitation has not picked up the slack; non-marital childbearing remains socially constrained. The framework calls this configuration an open action-space — a phantom-carrier zone where the canon has come down before any single replacement has compiled, but where the resulting pressure is productive friction driving multiple parallel experiments at once. The demographic numbers in that region are the most extreme on the planet, and the framework's reading is not that the system has failed but that East Asia is the region scouting hardest for what comes next.

Reading the regions

Nordic (CDI 9). Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland, Iceland. The legal parity between marriage and cohabitation is near-complete. The share of births outside marriage is the highest in the developed world: Iceland at 69%, Norway 61%, Sweden 58%, Denmark 55% as of recent OECD data. Among Swedes at age 35, 89–91% had started a first union as cohabitation and only 3% as marriage. Family stability — child outcomes, partnership duration, intergenerational support — does not appear to differ materially from married households. The framework's reading: this is the cleanest existing case of the institution's function being absorbed by other carriers (legal protection by statute, child-rearing infrastructure by the welfare state) such that the institution itself has become optional rather than load-bearing. Muscle memory phase.

Western Europe (CDI 8). France, Netherlands, Belgium, Germany, the British Isles. France's PACS civil-partnership system, introduced in 1999, has produced 209,800 civil unions against 241,700 marriages in 2022 — roughly 87% of marriage volume, and exceeding marriage in 2020 during COVID. France's share of non-marital births was 65.2% in 2023, the highest in Europe. The Netherlands and Belgium have parallel structures. Cohabitation is socially indistinguishable from marriage for most purposes. The institution is no longer the only carrier, but it remains a culturally significant one.

Southern Europe (CDI 6). Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions retain symbolic weight; marriage rates have fallen sharply (Italy 3.1 per 1,000 in 2023, a long-running decline) but cohabitation is still less institutionalized than in the north. Italy's share of non-marital births reached 43.2% in 2024 — up 23.5 percentage points since 2008 — placing it near the EU average of 41.1%. Birth rates are very low (Spain TFR 1.10 in 2024), but for reasons more closely tied to economic precarity and housing access than to direct dissolution of the institution.

Eastern Europe and Russia (CDI 4). Marriage remains a more normative aspiration but rates have fallen and divorce rates are high. Cohabitation is rising but slower than in the West. Demographic trends are negative; the canon is wobbling rather than refactoring.

North America (CDI 6). The United States and Canada. The US marriage rate has fallen roughly 65% from its 1970 peak; 25% of 40-year-olds in the US had never married as of 2021. Cohabitation has crossed marriage as a first-union form — 59% of adults 18–44 have ever cohabited vs 50% who have ever been married, and 76% of marriages today are preceded by cohabitation. Non-marital births are around 40% in the US, of which roughly 60% occur to cohabiting couples. A class divide is increasingly visible: marriage persists more strongly among the college-educated, while non-marital child-rearing has risen sharply in lower-income strata. This is a heterogeneous CDI 6 with substantial internal variance.

Latin America (CDI 7). Colombia leads with 87% of births outside marriage — the highest share recorded for any major country. Peru, Brazil, Venezuela, and others share the deep regional pattern of uniones consensuales — long-standing consensual unions that pre-date European-style formal marriage and have always been an institutionally separate carrier. The 2024 update from CDI 6 to CDI 7 reflects how striking the Colombian figure looks once one sees it next to the Nordics. Argentina and Chile still resemble Southern Europe, but the regional center of mass is closer to the Nordic muscle-memory shape than to a transition state — with one important asterisk: the absorbing carrier is consensual union as a folk institution, not the welfare-state-backed cohabitation of the Nordic model. The function is being met; the structural backing is different.

South Asia (CDI 2). India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka. Marriage is near-universal. A 2018 survey of 160,000+ households found 93% of marriages were family-arranged; more recent surveys show that share falling (arranged-marriage proportion dropped from 68% in 2020 to 44% in 2023 in one survey, though regional variation is wide). Reported divorce rates remain very low (1–2% for arranged marriages, 20–30% for the smaller love-marriage cohort). The institution is the load-bearing carrier for paternity certainty, property transfer, alliance, and child-rearing — the original squeeze functions, mostly unrelieved by alternative carriers.

MENA (CDI 1). The Middle East and North Africa. Religious law remains the operating canon for personal status in most countries; wholly Shari'a-based systems govern family matters in Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Iran, and Iraq. Cohabitation is criminalized in many places, though so-called "white marriages" — unregistered cohabitation — have been quietly spreading in capital cities like Tehran despite strong religious disapproval and legal obstacles. Divorce rates have roughly doubled in Egypt, Iran, and Saudi Arabia between 2004 and 2020. The institution is the most heavily reinforced and the least dissolved of any region on the map, but the early stress signals are visible.

Sub-Saharan Africa (CDI 3). The most internally heterogeneous region. Customary marriage forms — bridewealth, polygyny, matrilineal kinship in some areas — coexist with religious marriages (Christian and Muslim) and civil registration. DHS data from 27 sub-Saharan countries show median polygyny prevalence dropping roughly 29% since the 1990s (about 2% per year), with steeper falls in higher-HDI countries (South Africa now around 2%) and persistent prevalence above 30% in the rural belt from Senegal to Tanzania. The institution is load-bearing but the form varies dramatically across countries. Some urban areas resemble a CDI 5; rural and traditional areas remain CDI 2–3.

Southeast Asia (CDI 4). Wide variation. The Philippines and Indonesia retain strong religious frameworks; Thailand and Vietnam have higher cohabitation acceptance; Singapore resembles East Asia's pattern. The regional average disguises a substantial spread.

Oceania (CDI 7). Australia and New Zealand resemble Western Europe in legal parity and cohabitation rates. Marriage equality legislation removed a major exclusionary boundary in the 2010s. The institution is one carrier among several.

East Asia (Open action-space). Japan, South Korea, China, Taiwan. The institution is dissolving fast. Japan recorded 720,988 births in 2024 — the lowest figure since record-keeping began in 1899, the ninth consecutive annual decline. South Korea's total fertility rate fell to 0.65 in late 2023 and stood at 0.75 in 2024, the lowest in the world for the fifth consecutive year; women now marry for the first time at an average age of 31.6, men at 33.9 (both record highs). China registered 6.106 million marriages in 2024, down 20.5% year-on-year and 52.5% from the 2013 peak of roughly 13 million. The social and legal context in all three countries still constrains non-marital childbearing, and the labor-market and housing structures make couple-formation expensive. The result is that the functions marriage was performing — labor pooling, child-rearing infrastructure, lineage continuation, elder care — are not being absorbed by a single alternative carrier on the timeline the institution dissolved on. The framework reads this not as a failure but as the substrate doing exactly what the framework's catalyst principle predicts: where the canon dissolves before a successor compiles, a phantom action-space opens, and the resulting friction drives the population to scout multiple parallel candidate carriers at once. §06 walks through the four candidate carriers visible from where we stand in 2026. East Asia is the region scouting hardest because the friction is most acute there.

04The four substrates that lock the canon in

The procedural-lineage rendering from continuation 18 says that any canon at the social-substrate ring is built on top of the rings beneath: biology, then cognition, then earlier social compiles. Marriage as canon is shaped by four substrates that wrap around it and either reinforce it or release it. Reading the regional map without these substrates produces a flat-looking distribution. Reading the map through them shows why each region sits where it sits.

Religion

Where religious authority is still the operating canon for personal status — much of MENA, parts of South Asia, evangelical pockets in sub-Saharan Africa and the Americas — marriage remains sacrosanct because its rules are inherited unbroken from earlier compiles that were never refactored at the religious ring. The institution is wrapped in a meaning layer that any alternative carrier would have to displace, not merely supplement. Where religion has loosened its grip on personal status (Northern Europe, secular-leaning East Asia, parts of Latin America), the institution can dissolve faster because its meaning layer is no longer load-bearing.

Climate

Harsh climates have historically demanded the highest coordination cost for household formation, and marriage was the protocol that locked in the labor pooling required to survive. Pastoral and high-latitude pre-industrial societies had the densest marriage-and-kin protocols. Tropical and resource-abundant pre-industrial societies often had looser ones. As climate-resilience infrastructure (heating, food storage, transport, building stock) has improved, the coordination cost has fallen, and the canon's load-bearing has eased. The Nordic case is partially climate-substrate-driven: the cold made the squeeze hard, the welfare state distilled the muscle memory, and the institution could relax.

Government

States that have taken over the original squeeze functions — pensions, childcare, healthcare, inheritance-by-statute, property registration, paternity testing — have, over a few generations, made marriage operationally redundant for households that interact mainly with the state. States that have not taken over these functions (much of MENA, parts of South Asia, fragile-state contexts) leave marriage doing the original work, because no alternative carrier is available. The Nordic CDI 9 is in large part a story about what a strong welfare state will absorb if you let it. The MENA CDI 1 is in large part a story about what happens when the state does not absorb those functions and religious law continues to.

Ideology

Each region runs an ideological frame about what marriage is for. Individualist frames (Northern Europe, the Anglosphere, urban East Asia) make the institution optional once the romantic-partnership function can be met without it. Collectivist or familist frames (South Asia, MENA, much of sub-Saharan Africa, traditional Latin America) treat marriage as a node in a kin graph whose obligations extend well beyond the couple, which makes it much harder to dissolve because the dissolution would disconnect the graph. The East Asian misfire is partly an ideology-substrate problem: the familist frame remains strong enough that non-marital childbearing is socially expensive, but the institution itself has become economically and personally costly enough that people opt out. Neither side of the frame is bearing the load.

The four substrates do not act independently. They interlock. The Nordic CDI 9 is a configuration: low religious authority over personal status + post-industrial climate-resilience infrastructure + strong welfare state + individualist ideology, all reinforcing each other. The MENA CDI 1 is the opposite configuration on all four. Most of the map is the messy middle, where one or two substrates are pulling the canon one direction and the others are holding it in place.

05From squeeze to pull: the muscle-memory transition

The Nordic case is worth slowing down for, because it shows the framework's claim most cleanly.

Sweden in 1900 had marriage rates and family structures broadly typical of Northern European Lutheran societies. The institution was load-bearing for paternity, inheritance, labor pooling, child-rearing, alliance, and elder care — the full squeeze stack. Across the 20th century, each of the squeeze functions was absorbed by a different carrier. Universal public schools took over much of the child-development load. The welfare state's pension system took over elder care. Inheritance law was rewritten to treat cohabiting partners equivalently to spouses for many purposes. Universal healthcare detached survival from household resource pooling. The kindergarten and after-school systems took over the day-care portion of child-rearing. The result, by the 1990s, was that almost none of the original squeezes were still being absorbed by marriage. They had migrated.

And yet — and this is the part that matters — the practical knowledge of how to run a stable two-adult partnership did not migrate with them. It distilled. Couples in Sweden cohabit at very high rates, raise children at family-stability levels comparable to or better than marrying couples, and divide labor and finances in arrangements that look procedurally identical to marriages of two generations earlier minus the ceremony. The institution dissolved; the muscle memory remained. The protocol no longer needs the canon to carry it, because the protocol has been absorbed into the population's default behavior.

This is what the framework calls the squeeze-to-pull transition. A squeeze produces a canon. The canon distributes a protocol through enough generations that the protocol becomes legible without the canon being explicitly invoked. When that happens, the canon can lighten its hold — or pass into dormancy — without the protocol being lost. The squeeze gave way to a pull: people don't need the institution to do the thing the institution was designed to make them do. And the framework's v1.2 refinement: the dormancy is not a death. If the squeeze ever returns, the canon can wake. When it wakes, it will not be the same canon — it will be refactored, carrying whatever the dormancy period imparted. Reversibility is built into canon-formation in a way that other models of institutional change usually skip.

The key check on this reading is the family-stability data. If Nordic cohabitation produced systematically worse child outcomes, weaker elder care, or higher household instability than marriage, the muscle-memory claim would be wrong; the canon would still be doing load-bearing work, and we'd just be watching that work fail. The evidence does not show that. By most family-outcome measures the Nordics are at the top of the developed world. This is consistent with the muscle-memory reading. It does not prove it.

This is what the framework would call a successful canon dormancy. The institution did its job — well enough, for long enough — that the population learned what it was teaching, and the canon can now pass into a sleeping state with the imparted learning carrying its protocol forward. That is rare. Most canon does not get that far. Most canon either persists past its usefulness (the squeeze-era extremes on our map) or passes into an open action-space where the original function is now scouting for a successor across multiple candidate carriers in parallel.

The open-action-space case is East Asia, and it is one of the most generative configurations on the map. The next section walks through what is being scouted there.

06The open action-space: where the next carrier gets scouted

The Nordic case shows what a clean squeeze-to-pull transition looks like — the institution relaxes into muscle memory, the function is preserved, the population doesn't notice the change because nothing was ever lost. The East Asian case shows something else, equally important, that the framework's vocabulary names but does not call a failure. The squeeze relaxed faster than the substrates underneath were ready to absorb it. The institution dissolved without a single ready successor. What follows is not absence; it is the most acute productive friction on the planet, and it is driving the most aggressive multi-carrier search-and-compile happening anywhere in the developed world.

The framework's catalyst principle (continuation 19) says external substrate is the framework's growth metabolism — the friction that comes from the canon no longer covering the action is what makes the system reach for new carriers. The misfire frame would say: the canon broke and nothing arrived. The action-space frame says: the canon dissolved, the function is now an open problem on the substrate, and the system is doing what it should — running parallel scouts on every candidate that might absorb the load. The numbers from the previous section are not a failure signal; they are the pressure gradient that's driving the search.

Japan recorded 720,988 births in 2024, the lowest since record-keeping began in 1899, and the ninth consecutive annual decline. South Korea's total fertility rate stood at 0.65–0.75 across 2023–2024, less than half of replacement, the lowest in the world for the fifth consecutive year. China's 2024 marriage registrations fell 20.5% year-on-year to 6.1 million, less than half of the 2013 peak of roughly 13 million — and the marriage-age (20–39) population itself shrunk from about 435 million in 2013 to roughly 371 million by 2023. Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore follow the same curve. From a framework perspective, these are not a verdict; they are the input signal driving the scouting.

Four candidate carriers currently being scouted

Read against the original six squeeze functions marriage was solving (paternity, inheritance, child-rearing infrastructure, alliance, labor pooling, elder care), four candidate carriers are visibly in motion across Japan, South Korea, and China — and elsewhere, but most acutely there. None of them has compiled into canon. Each one is at a different stage of the carrier-before-canon cycle the framework's continuation 16 named.

One. Robotics in the workforce and the household. Japan has been the world leader in industrial robotics for two generations and is the global frontier of social and care robotics. Honda's Asimo lineage, Softbank's Pepper, paro the therapeutic robot seal in dementia-ward use, robotic exoskeletons for elderly mobility, automated checkout and food-prep deployed nationally, robot bartenders, robot receptionists. South Korea is close behind; China is now scaling humanoid robotics under explicit state policy as a response to its own demographic curve. The function being absorbed here is the household-as-production-unit half of the original squeeze. When a robot can do laundry, prepare meals, monitor an elderly parent, and tutor a child, the labor-pooling function marriage compiled around becomes optional in a way it has never been before. The carrier is being built but the canon has not formed yet; the protocols of household division-of-labor between humans and machines are unstable and culturally negotiated case by case.

Two. Active longevity via medicine and genetic intervention. If life expectancy in active health extends from the current ~75 to 120 or beyond — which the GLP-1 / rapamycin / NAD+ / Yamanaka-factor / senolytic clusters are now plausibly aiming at — the timing constraints that built the squeeze relax entirely. The reason marriage compiled around early-life partnership formation and child-rearing is that the productive window was short. Extend the window, and the urgency of compressing partnership, childbearing, and child-rearing into the same decade dissolves. Japan and South Korea are running this experiment not by intention but by population structure: they are the world's largest demographic laboratory for what happens when very long active life becomes a planning premise rather than a hope. The relevant longevity carriers are at different maturities — GLP-1 is mainstream, rapamycin is research-stage, gene editing is regulatory — but the directional vector is one.

Three. AI symbiosis as companion, co-parent, network-builder. Japanese AI-companion apps and "virtual marriage" services predate the Western Replika / Character.AI wave and are deeply embedded in Japanese single-adult life. South Korea has rapid uptake of AI-tutoring and AI-eldercare. China's deployment of AI in education and welfare is the largest in the world by scale. The function being scouted here is partly the romantic-partnership emotional layer marriage carried, partly the always-available collaborator-and-witness role, partly the eldercare-attention layer, and — at the speculative end — a co-parenting interface that could plausibly absorb part of the cognitive load of child-rearing. AI symbiosis is the youngest carrier of the four and the one where the canon is least formed; what's compiling now is the basic etiquette and contract layer between humans and persistent AI companions.

Four. New protocols and institutions. Less futuristic and arguably the most empirically advanced. Japan has an enormous solo-wedding industry, growing "kazoku no katachi" experiments (new family forms), pet-as-family-member formalization, and increasing legal recognition of partnership outside marriage. South Korea's "honjok" (one-person-household) culture is now the dominant household type. China has rising one-person households, urban "rented-family" rituals around New Year, and emerging legal frameworks for partnership outside marriage. These are not the dissolution of family; they are the explicit construction of new social contracts that absorb pieces of the original marriage function without recompiling it as one institution. The carrier is the new contract template; the canon is the social norm cluster that gives the template traction.

These four are not independent. Robotics depends on AI symbiosis to be useful in the home. Active longevity changes the time-horizon assumptions all four carriers compile against. New protocols and institutions either help or block the others depending on how they're drafted. The framework's reading is that what's running in East Asia is a four-way coupled experiment in which the new canon — whatever shape it takes — will be the compile of whichever combination produces the most family-stability per unit of friction across the four substrates.

Two important caveats. First, the framework is not claiming any of the four will succeed at the population level on the current timeline; the demographic numbers say none of them is currently absorbing the function fast enough to stabilize birth rates. Second, the four candidates are not value-neutral — each one comes with its own substrate costs (robot dependency, longevity inequality, AI-mediated isolation, fragmented contract complexity). The framework's claim is structural, not advisory: this is what an open action-space looks like when the original canon dissolves before a successor is in place. Productive friction does not guarantee a good outcome; it guarantees that scouting is happening at maximum intensity. What the scouting compiles into is the next round's question.

The Nordic and East Asian cases are not opposite ends of a single dimension. They are two different exits from the squeeze-era equilibrium. The Nordics already had a successor carrier compiling alongside the institution (welfare-state-backed cohabitation) and got to dissolve the institution into muscle memory. East Asia is dissolving the institution first and scouting the successor in the wake of the dissolution. The Nordic exit is more comfortable. The East Asian exit is generating more new structure per year, because the friction is higher.

If the framework's reading is right, the policy question for open-action-space regions is not "how do we get people to marry again." It is "which of the four candidate carriers is your jurisdiction actively investing in, which is your jurisdiction blocking by default, and which combination is most likely to absorb the original squeeze functions without producing worse substrate costs of its own." The Nordic answer was a single coupled solution (welfare state plus distilled cohabitation). The East Asian answer will be a four-vector mixture — and the mixture will look different in Japan than in South Korea than in China, because their substrate stacks are different.

07Three speculative bubbles

Each of these is a candidate-grade reading — a claim the framework would make based on the squeeze-to-pull lens, attached to a follow-up plan that would either corroborate or break it. The rank is the framework's current confidence ordering. Sources for each are in the references section.

Bubble 01 rank 1 · highest confidence

The Nordic distillation hypothesis

In societies that have run the squeeze-to-pull transition cleanly, the original child-rearing function of marriage is being met at parity by cohabiting unions because the state has absorbed the carrier functions (pension, healthcare, childcare, inheritance protection) and the population has absorbed the partnership protocol as default behavior. The institution has become optional without the function being lost.

What it predicts Family-stability outcomes (child wellbeing, partnership duration, elder-care quality) in Nordic countries should not differ materially between married and cohabiting households of comparable socio-economic status — and the gap that does exist should narrow further over the next decade.

What would break it A re-widening of the gap, or evidence that Nordic non-marital partnerships are systematically less stable in ways the gross statistics have been masking.

Follow-up Pull longitudinal data from Nordic statistical agencies (SCB, SSB, DST) on child outcomes by parental marital status, controlling for income and education. Repeat in 12 months and again in 36 months. Target measure: standard deviation of partnership-duration distribution and a child-poverty rate by household type.

Bubble 02 rank 2 · medium confidence

The East Asian open-action-space hypothesis

Where the institution dissolves before a single successor carrier is in place, a phantom action-space opens at the social-substrate ring and the population scouts multiple candidate carriers in parallel: robotics (Japan/Korea/China leading globally), active longevity medicine (the demographic curve makes this a state-priority research investment), AI symbiosis (Japan ahead on companion-and-eldercare AI, China ahead on scale deployment), and explicit new protocols/institutions (honjok, kazoku-no-katachi, formal solo-and-pet households). The framework predicts the next compiled canon at this ring will be a coupled combination of these four — not any one of them alone — and the combination will differ across Japan, South Korea, and China because their substrate stacks differ.

What it predicts By 2040 at least two of the four candidate carriers will have crossed into recognizable canon status in at least one of the three countries — visible as state-acknowledged policy frameworks, mainstream household configurations, and measurable family-stability outcomes inside the new arrangements. Direct pronatalist financial incentives alone, without infrastructural investment in at least one of the four, will continue to fail. South Korea has spent ~$280 billion on pro-natalist incentives since 2006 and its TFR has continued to fall from 1.13 to 0.65 — the cleanest existing data point that incentive-without-carrier-investment does not work. One wrinkle worth tracking: South Korean nuptials rose 14.9% in 2024, the sharpest single-year jump since 1970. This is plausibly post-COVID rebound noise, but it is also the first signal the system is producing in the carrier-search direction and is worth distinguishing from either noise or precursor-of-something.

What would break it By 2040, none of the four candidates has compiled into recognizable canon in any of the three countries; the institution-of-marriage either reasserts as the operating carrier or the function is genuinely abandoned at the population level. Either outcome would refute the open-action-space framing and recover the misfire interpretation as the correct one.

Follow-up Track four indicators across Japan, South Korea, and China at 12, 36, and 60 months: (a) robot installations per 10,000 industrial workers and per 10,000 households; (b) median healthy-life-expectancy and GLP-1 / senolytic / longevity-intervention adoption rates; (c) AI companion / care-AI penetration rates by household type; (d) legal recognition and uptake of non-marital partnership and single-household contractual forms. The framework expects all four to rise in parallel; if one is dominant and the others flat, that is information about which carrier is winning the local scouting.

Bubble 03 rank 3 · lower confidence, larger consequence if true

The climate-coupling hypothesis

The canon-dissolution index of a region in the long run is partially a function of how much climate-resilience infrastructure that region has built. The harsher the historical climate squeeze, the denser the original marriage canon; the more the squeeze has been relieved by infrastructure (heating, food storage, transport, building stock, social safety net), the faster the canon can dissolve without functional cost. As climate change reverses some of that infrastructure's effectiveness — making conditions harsher again in some regions — the framework predicts a partial re-tightening of marriage canon in the affected regions, especially in middle-income countries without strong welfare-state backstops.

What it predicts In regions where climate stress is increasing (parts of the Sahel, South Asia, low-lying Southeast Asia, climate-vulnerable Latin American zones), the marriage and kinship canon should either stop softening or, in extreme cases, stiffen — as households re-aggregate to absorb the coordination cost the welfare state was supposed to cover and increasingly cannot.

What would break it Continued softening of marriage canon in climate-stressed middle-income regions over the next decade, despite welfare-state degradation. That would suggest the canon is dissolving on a different timeline than the climate-resilience substrate, weakening the coupling claim.

Follow-up Track the IPCC climate-vulnerability index against marriage-rate change for a panel of ~30 middle-income countries over the next 5 and 10 years. The framework expects a positive correlation between climate stress and marriage-canon re-tightening; absence of correlation, or the opposite sign, would break the bubble.

All three bubbles are speculations the framework's reading produces. None of them is canon yet. The agnostic discipline says they have to survive a check before they can compile. The point of writing them down is so that the check can be run — by anyone, including the framework itself in twelve and thirty-six months — and so that what survives can be promoted, while what doesn't can be pruned and kept on the failed-reading shelf as honest provenance.

08What this case study is doing for the framework

Marriage is a good worked example for the canon-dissolution dynamic because the data is unusually well-recorded (most jurisdictions have kept marriage and birth registries for over a century), the regional variation is large enough to compare, the four substrates (religion, climate, government, ideology) are all separately observable, and the timeline is slow enough that we can see compile and dissolution within recent memory. If the squeeze-to-pull primitive is real at the social-substrate ring, marriage is one of the places it should be visible.

The reading also stress-tests two of the framework's recent canon entries. The procedural-lineage rendering from continuation 18 should make sense of why the four substrates interlock — biology (child obligate-helplessness) wraps cognition (paternity uncertainty, alliance reasoning) wraps society (kin networks, inheritance, alliance) wraps person (the romantic and partnership feeling). Take a layer away, the layers above wobble. The Nordic distillation works because the lower layers got new substrates underneath them; the East Asian misfire fails because the substrates didn't all migrate together. If the procedural-lineage primitive is right, this is exactly the shape we should expect a canon-dissolution event to take. So far the marriage case is consistent.

The Lakatosian success criterion from continuation 19 is the right evaluation game for a reading like this. The point is not whether the canon-dissolution-index map gets every region exactly right. The point is whether the framework's vocabulary lets us see structure that other readings miss — the open-action-space category as a distinct configuration from squeeze and pull, the four-substrate interlock, the muscle-memory transition as the test of a successful canon dissolution, and the productive-friction framing of regions like East Asia that other readings treat as failure. Those are operational moves. They give policy and personal-life purchase: if you want the institution to relax without the function being lost, either the successor carriers have to be built ahead of dissolution (the Nordic answer) or you have to accept and steer the multi-carrier scouting that follows dissolution (the East Asian present). That is a recommendation derivable from the framework that is not derivable from the canonical sociology-of-marriage literature in the same form. Whether it survives further testing is a different question. The first question is whether it makes a move you couldn't make without it.

The honest weakness of this reading: the CDI numbers are framework-graded composites, not a single measured index. The underlying signals — marriage rate, non-marital birth share, age at first marriage, legal parity, divorce rate — are individually sourced (OECD Family Database, UN Population Division, Eurostat, national statistical agencies, Insee/INED, Korean KOSIS, China NBS), but the synthesis into a single 0–10 score is the framework's compile, with different weights producing different rankings. The v1 to v1-revised update bumped Latin America from CDI 6 to CDI 7 after the Colombia figure (87% births outside marriage) came in higher than the framework had originally graded. That kind of revision is exactly the work the framework is supposed to be doing: keep the discipline, update the values when the underlying signals contradict them. The bubbles inherit that uncertainty too — they are candidate-grade, not canon.

09Adjacent fields and leading figures

The framework is not the first reading to notice that marriage as institution is dissolving in the developed world, nor the first to ask what is absorbing its function. The squeeze-to-pull / muscle-memory framing sits next to several established literatures, each of which names a piece of the same shape. Worth tracing them — partly to credit the precedents, partly to give a reader who finds this useful entry points into the deeper work, and partly so the framework's own claims can be checked against fields that have been running their own A⁻ on these questions for decades.

Before the field map, one terminological note. Cultural osmosis — the indirect, ambient absorption of social knowledge through environment rather than instruction — is a real anthropological concept. It is the broad umbrella under which several specific dynamics sit, and it accurately describes part of what's happening in the marriage case (the way a Nordic child absorbs the cohabitation-as-default protocol without being explicitly taught it). But cultural osmosis is too loose for the specific squeeze-and-distill cycle the framework is naming. Two more precise terms come from family demography and demographic transition theory respectively, and a third comes from cultural evolution. They are the closest established analogues, and the framework's contribution is best understood as a refactor sitting alongside them rather than a replacement.

1. Family demography · deinstitutionalization of marriage

The closest single-concept parallel to the framework's reading is Andrew Cherlin's "deinstitutionalization of marriage" (2004 paper of that title; expanded in The Marriage-Go-Round, 2009). Cherlin argues that marriage in the West has shifted from an institutional carrier of social roles (provider, homemaker, parent) toward an expressive carrier of personal achievement and identity — and that this shift is exactly what makes its dissolution possible without the family-stability function being lost in the same step. The framework's "squeeze-to-pull" is essentially Cherlin's "institutional-to-expressive-to-optional" with the substrate-stack analysis added underneath.

Other figures worth knowing in this literature: Stephanie Coontz (Evergreen State College; Marriage, a History, 2005) on the historical case that marriage as romantic-partnership institution is recent and that earlier marriage was primarily economic; Sara McLanahan (Princeton, deceased 2021) on the class-divide reading of the US case; Wendy Manning and Susan Brown (Bowling Green State University, National Center for Family & Marriage Research) on US cohabitation patterns; Larry Bumpass (Wisconsin) on the long-running Wisconsin demographic data.

2. Second Demographic Transition (SDT)

Ron Lesthaeghe (Vrije Universiteit Brussel, emeritus) and Dirk van de Kaa (1933–2014) named the Second Demographic Transition in 1986. Their thesis: the contraceptive, sexual, and women's-rights revolutions of the 1960s produced a distinct demographic regime characterized by persistently low fertility, delayed marriage and childbearing, rising non-marital cohabitation and childbearing, and high divorce rates. SDT theory explicitly characterizes this as a weakening of the family institution, and Lesthaeghe's later work tracks it across regions including parts of Asia.

What the framework adds to SDT: (a) the misfire category as a distinct configuration from successful transition — SDT theory treats East Asia as a stalled or late case, the framework treats it as a different shape entirely; (b) the substrate-stack analysis (religion / climate / government / ideology) as the explanation for why the transition runs cleanly in some regions and stalls in others; (c) the muscle-memory frame as the criterion for a successful canon dissolution rather than just a sequence of behavioral changes.

Other figures: Brienna Perelli-Harris (University of Southampton) on cross-national non-marital childbearing trends; Tomáš Sobotka (Vienna Institute of Demography) on lowest-low fertility; Gavin Jones (NUS) on Asian marriage transitions.

3. Cultural evolution and dual inheritance theory

Robert Boyd (Arizona State University) and Peter Richerson (UC Davis, emeritus) founded dual-inheritance theory in Culture and the Evolutionary Process (1985). Their model: humans inherit both a genotype and a body of socially-learned information, and the cultural body evolves through its own Darwinian-like selection process — with transmission biases (content bias, conformist bias, prestige bias) determining which variants spread.

The framework's carrier-vs-canon distinction maps directly onto Boyd-Richerson's cultural variants vs cultural pool. The framework's procedural-lineage (cont 18) is most closely related to Joseph Henrich's work — particularly The Secret of Our Success (2015) and The WEIRDest People in the World (2020), which reads the medieval Western Church's Marriage-Family Program as a multi-century cultural-evolutionary intervention that produced the modern Western individualist marriage canon. Henrich's MFP thesis is the cleanest historical-cultural-evolution account of how the specific marriage canon being dissolved today got compiled in the first place.

Adjacent figures: Kevin Laland (St Andrews) on cultural niche construction — closest formal-theoretical parallel to the framework's substrate-as-procedural-lineage; Alex Mesoudi (Exeter) on experimental cultural evolution; Luigi Cavalli-Sforza (1922–2018) and Marcus Feldman (Stanford) on the mathematical foundations.

4. Kinship anthropology (newer wave)

Classical kinship anthropology — Lewis Henry Morgan's 19th-century lineage taxonomies, Lévi-Strauss's mid-20th-century alliance theory — treated kinship as a relatively rigid structural system. The newer wave, starting from the 1990s, refactored kinship as a flexible cultural compile that varies more by what people actively do than by what they're born into.

Janet Carsten (University of Edinburgh) is the central figure of this turn, with Cultures of Relatedness (2000) and After Kinship (2004). She introduced relatedness as a term meant to replace kinship for analyses of how people make and dissolve family ties through shared experience, food, residence, and care — not just blood and contract. The framework's "carriers compile when shared experience compiles, dissolve when it doesn't" reading is closely adjacent to Carsten's relatedness frame.

Adjacent figures: Marilyn Strathern (Cambridge, emerita) on reproductive technologies and the gift economy of family-making; Sarah Franklin (Cambridge) on IVF, surrogacy, and how kinship is being refactored at the technology ring; Susan McKinnon (University of Virginia) on cross-cultural critiques of evolutionary-psychology accounts of kinship; David Schneider (1918–1995) for the foundational mid-20th-century critique of universalizing American kinship categories.

5. Historical sociology of marriage and the family

Göran Therborn (Cambridge, emeritus) — Between Sex and Power: Family in the World 1900–2000 (2004) — produced the most ambitious cross-regional history of 20th-century family forms, tracking patriarchy, fertility regimes, and marriage institutions across all major world regions. His regional taxonomy is one direct ancestor of the heat-map approach this essay uses.

Adjacent figures: Mary Ann Glendon (Harvard Law) on the comparative legal history of marriage; John Hajnal (1924–2008) for the Hajnal Line distinguishing Western European late-marriage patterns from the rest of pre-modern Europe (a structural-substrate fact the framework's substrate-stack inherits without yet citing explicitly); Kingsley Davis (1908–1997) for the first demographic-transition framing on which Lesthaeghe and van de Kaa built.

6. East Asian demography (the open-action-space specialists)

The misfire category in this essay is the framework's own naming, but the underlying empirical work is a specialist literature in its own right. Mary Brinton (Harvard) is the leading sociologist of Japanese family change, with Women and the Economic Miracle (1993) and subsequent work on the institutional reasons Japanese women in particular have withdrawn from marriage. Wang Feng (UC Irvine) is the leading demographer on Chinese marriage and family change. James Raymo (Princeton) works on the cross-national comparison of low-fertility societies. Hyunjoon Park (Penn) and Eun-Kyung Kim work on Korean family transitions. Stuart Gietel-Basten (HKUST) covers the East Asian lowest-low-fertility cluster comparatively.

Anne Allison (Duke) takes the anthropological angle on Japanese precarity, work, and family. Frances Rosenbluth (Yale, deceased 2021) wrote with Margarita Estévez-Abe on how labor-market and gender-norm interactions explain the East Asian fertility collapse. The open-action-space reading in this essay — that East Asia is scouting candidate carriers in parallel rather than failing to absorb the function — is the framework's compile over their data, not a claim those scholars themselves make in those words. v1 of this essay framed the same configuration as a "misfire"; that framing was corrected in v1.1 because it imported a failure-frame that is not in the framework's own vocabulary. What the friction is producing is the open question; calling it failure prejudges it.

What the framework's contribution actually is

Read against these six adjacent fields, the framework is not introducing the dormancy of marriage as a new observation. It is doing four specific moves on top of what's already established:

One. Unification across substrates. The biology of endosymbiotic tension (Lane, Margulis), the cultural-evolution view of carriers (Boyd, Richerson, Henrich), the family-demography view of institutional dissolution (Cherlin, Lesthaeghe), and the kinship-anthropology view of relatedness (Carsten) are normally siloed. The framework's procedural-lineage primitive (cont 18) is what licenses reading them as the same shape at different rings. Whether that licensing holds is the open question; the unification claim is the part most likely to be wrong, and also the part that has the most operational purchase if it survives.

Two. Naming the open-action-space category. SDT theory treats East Asia as late or stalled; the framework treats it as a distinct configuration where the canon dissolves before a single successor carrier is in place and where productive friction drives a parallel multi-carrier search (robotics, active longevity, AI symbiosis, new protocols/institutions). This is a small refactor on existing demography, but it changes the policy implication from "wait for the transition to complete" or "the system has failed" to "track which of the four candidate carriers is being invested in, which is being blocked, and which combination is on track to compile."

Three. The muscle-memory criterion as imparted learning. Existing literatures track behavioral change. The framework adds the question: did the protocol distill into the population's default behavior (the imparted learning that persists during canon dormancy), or did the canon pass out of load-bearing without the protocol being preserved (open action-space)? The same question now includes the v1.2 refinement: if the canon ever wakes — and dormancy is reversible by definition — it returns refactored, carrying whatever the dormancy imparted. That reframes the Nordic and East Asian cases not as success-vs-failure on a single curve but as two different dormancy configurations that would wake differently if the squeeze re-emerged.

Four. The substrate-stack as diagnostic. Asking which of religion, climate, government, and ideology is doing the load-bearing in each region gives a faster purchase on why dormancy looks different in different places than the standard regional-comparison literature provides. It also predicts where additional dormancy is or isn't likely, based on which substrates are shifting.

None of these moves is original to the agnostic framework in the sense of being absent from the adjacent literatures — each one has antecedents in the work of at least one of the figures above. What the framework is claiming is that the four moves are the same move, expressible in one vocabulary, and that the vocabulary's value is the cross-substrate purchase. That claim is exactly the kind of Lakatosian growth-test continuation 19 named: it has to be checked by how much new structure it makes visible, not by whether it forbids anything novel.
