## A small piece of magic

> *"There's another bit of magic that I have. I can get fire from a lighter when no one else can. At first there was a trick to it — to cup a palm over the lighter and collect the gas — but after a while, after people have tried and given up, I can just get a light without the ritual. To them it looks like magic. To me it's a hidden protocol I learned to read."*

Almost everyone has one of these. A trick that works in your hands and looks like magic in someone else's. The shower whose handle you've learned to nudge in just the right way. The car door that opens if you lift while you turn. The colleague who only stops talking if you ask exactly the right question. To you these are not mysterious. To anyone watching, they look like access to something they can't quite see.

The Agnostic framework starts there. Not with grand theory. With the recognition that hidden protocols are everywhere, that some of them you have learned to read and most of them you haven't, and that the world you live in is shaped much more by these invisible operating procedures than by anything written down.

It is also worth saying, this early, what these protocols actually are. A hidden protocol is not a static rule. It is *sequence knowledge* — a learned ordering of small moves where the order itself is the unlock. The lighter trick works because the gas accumulates first and ignites second; reverse the order and you get nothing. The shower handle works because the nudge happens before the wait, not during it. The colleague stops talking because the question lands before the topic shifts, not after. The same individual pieces, executed in a different order, do not produce the same outcome. Once you start looking for sequence as the unlock rather than content, you find protocols everywhere — in conversations, in relationships, in institutions, in your own habits.

This is a thinking tool. It will not tell you what is true. It will give you a vocabulary for noticing how things become true — how ideas travel, stick, mutate, and win; why some true things vanish without a trace and some false things reshape civilizations; how you are, right now, the compressed output of more influences than you can count. And how to participate in that process more deliberately than you currently do.

I am not asking you to believe the framework. I am inviting you to scout it — to walk into the speculation it spawns and see what holds up in your own life.

## You are a renderer. You are also a receiver. And you have a body.

The first move the framework makes is the strangest one, so it is worth going slowly.

We usually treat the world as something that arrives at us — information comes in, we absorb it, we know things. But consider what actually happens when you see a face across a room and immediately feel something — like or dislike, trust or wariness. You did not consult evidence. You ran a fast pattern-match against everything you have ever experienced involving faces, voices, posture, context. The result felt like direct perception. It was not. It was a rendering.

That much is what most of cognitive science already says. The framework's move is to add the symmetry that most accounts skip:

> *"I have a swarm of inner selves... they get conjured up in the sandbox, however they are always active in the subconscious."*

You are not only rendering signals from outside. You are also *receiving* signals from inside. From the subconscious. From imagination. From dreams. From an intuition that arrives before the conscious knowing of it. These are not noise. They are real signals, traveling up into awareness from underneath, opening what the framework calls *phantom action-spaces* — possibility-paths that haven't been tested yet but feel weighty enough to attend to.

Most accounts of the mind treat conscious experience as the result of two operations: information coming in, and processing producing experience. The framework's claim is that there are two flows, not one. Outside signals come in. Inside signals come up. The conscious mind is where they meet and get sorted into what feels like reality.

Imagine, for a moment, your awareness as a small bright room. Light enters from one wall — that's the outside flow: senses, conversations, news, weather, other people. Light also rises from the floor — that's the inside flow: hunches, dreams, half-formed thoughts, the strange certainty about something you haven't seen evidence for yet. You are standing in the room. Both lights are real. Both are partial. Together they produce what you experience as a complete picture.

And there is one more thing in that room that v5 wants to name explicitly, because v4 left it implicit. The room has walls. The walls are made of *you* — specifically, your body. The body is not a transport mechanism for the mind; it is the outermost rendering membrane, the place where everything inside meets everything outside, the regulator that decides what gets in and what stays out. Tired walls render the world tired. Hungry walls render it threatening. Walls in love render the same situation as opportunity. The body is the renderer's first stage, not a separate thing the renderer happens to inhabit. Every signal that enters your awareness passes through it, and every signal that leaves your awareness passes through it again on the way back into the world.

This matters because most accounts of cognition treat the body as plumbing — a way to keep the brain alive while the real work happens elsewhere. The framework's view is that the body is the first node in the rendering network and the last. When you change what your body is doing — sleep, exercise, breath, hunger, posture — you do not "calm down" or "wake up" the same mind. You change the membrane the mind is running on, and the rendering shifts accordingly. The thing nobody told you, and the thing this framework insists on, is that the floor-light, the wall-light, and the membrane itself are all doing real work. Learning to read all three is the beginning of becoming a scout for yourself.

A small concrete version: notice that when two people are tracking the same conversation, they are doing what the framework calls *mutual rendering*. Your wall-light, their wall-light. Your floor-light, their floor-light. Your body, their body. The conversation that emerges in the middle is not a transmission; it is a co-construction. Neither of you sees the conversation that the other is seeing. What you share is the protocol — the agreed-upon sequence of moves that keeps two private renderings from drifting apart. When the protocol fails, you don't "miscommunicate." You diverge into two parallel scenes that no longer overlap.

## Speculation is the engine — but speculation alone is not enough

> *"Speculation is part of the process and selective barrier of how new kernel canon is created based on legacy. A phantom action space opens and then that gets mapped on legacy and evaluated as the next block or brick."*

Here is the move that most accounts of thinking miss entirely.

Speculation — the deliberate generation of possibilities you can't yet verify — is not a bug to be managed. It is the standard mechanism by which what you know extends itself. Every piece of canon you carry was once a speculation. Germ theory. The idea that another person can be trusted. The notion that you could be loved. The certainty that water boils at a particular temperature. All of these started as phantom action-spaces opened by subconscious or imagination, then mapped against your existing canon to see if they fit, then either accepted, rejected, or held in suspense.

The framework distinguishes four things people often confuse:

**Imagination** — the floor-light. Phantom action-spaces opening up. Possibilities you can hold in mind without committing to them. Children do this constantly. Adults often forget how.

**Hypothesis** — a speculation that has been named, made articulate, and proposed for testing. "I wonder if this is the case. Let me see what would count as evidence."

**Insight** — a speculation that survived feedback. It changed your map. You now run your life slightly differently because of it.

**Delusion** — a speculation that hardened into canon *before* feedback. The phantom action-space was treated as already-real before it earned that status.

The whole game is in the middle. Speculate freely. **Check seriously.** Prune ruthlessly. Accept what survives. If you suppress the speculation step entirely, you cut off the canon-extension mechanism and stop learning. If you let speculation run without checking, you slide into delusion. Neither extreme is stable. The framework names the discipline that operates between them.

But these two steps are not equal — and v4 of this manifesto made that asymmetry explicit, because it changes how you should hear everything that follows. Speculation is the easy half. Almost any system with enough capacity can generate plausible possibilities. The hard half — the discipline that actually forms canon — is the checking. The next section explains why, and what it means in practice.

The process worth naming here, because it underlies everything that follows, is the order in which you do the work:

1. **Intuit by yourself first.** Let speculation come up from below before you reach for the canon to test it against. Don't read the experts first. Sit with what your own floor-light is telling you. Develop your own version of the thing.

2. **Develop your inner canon.** Turn the speculation into something articulate enough to be checked. Give it provisional shape. Be specific about what it claims and what would count against it.

3. **Then bounce it off wider knowledge.** Now go read the people who came before. Find the places where your speculation already exists in better-developed form. Find the places where it doesn't and you might actually have something. Most of the time you'll discover your idea already has a name. Sometimes the name will surprise you. Occasionally — rarely — you'll find that no one else has put the pieces together quite the way you have. That's where contribution lives. In the synthesis, not the components.

If you flip the order — read the experts first, form your own view second — you import their canon before you've developed your own. You become an excellent rehearser of someone else's framework. You stop being a scout.

The reason this matters for the manifesto is that I am asking you, right now, to do the imagination step. To open phantom action-spaces around the ideas that follow. Not to believe them. To picture them. To see what holds in your own life when you turn the framework's vocabulary loose on situations you actually inhabit.

That is the agnostic purpose. Not to install a worldview. To spark the speculation function — and then point you at the harder half.

## The loop everything runs on

At any given moment, every observer — you, your friend, your employer, your country, the algorithm sorting your feed — is sitting somewhere between fuzzy and canon.

Fuzzy is the open territory. The things you have noticed but not yet integrated. The questions without answers. The situations where multiple interpretations are still on the table. Right now, some things in your mind are fuzzy: a relationship you have not yet figured out, a news story you only half-understood, a feeling you cannot quite name. Fuzzy is not a problem to be solved. It is the live state, the searching state, the state where learning is possible.

Canon is what has compiled. The things that survived enough testing that you are running them as operational assumptions. Your native language is canon. The floor being solid is canon. Your sense of whether you are a person people trust is canon. Canon does not mean eternal. It means load-bearing right now. The thing you are standing on while you reach for the next thing.

The loop that connects them is the framework's central engine. Something fuzzy catches attention — a gap, an anomaly, a nagging question. You reach: you ask someone, read something, try an experiment. Something comes back. **What survives the check** starts to compress into something portable: a word, a habit, a rule of thumb, a tool. The framework calls this a *carrier artifact*. It is now doing work for you without requiring constant attention. Enough carrier artifacts on the same subject eventually crystallize into a chunk of your operating canon.

> *"The give could be a new substrate aggregate compile when novel insight is gained."*

The word doing the most work in the paragraph above is *check*. v3 of this manifesto called it "coherence check" and moved on. v4 stopped there. v5 keeps the emphasis. Most of what feels like learning is generation — coming up with possibilities, reaching for ideas, having reactions. That part is easy and abundant. The thing that turns generation into knowledge is the checking step, where the candidate is matched against the substrate — against prior canon, against the world's response, against constraints you didn't choose. **What survives the check is what compiles.** Generation without checking is dreaming. Checking without generation is paralysis. Real canon-formation needs both, but it is the check that does the actual work.

A worthwhile reframing v5 wants to add: the loop is not a clean cycle that completes and resets. It runs in a *friction band*. Too little friction and the system fuses — every speculation becomes canon, nothing gets tested, everything is true and therefore nothing is. Too much friction and the system freezes — no speculation survives the check, nothing new compiles, the observer becomes a museum of its own past beliefs. Real learning happens in a narrow band between freeze and fuse, where friction is high enough that bad speculations die and low enough that good ones can land. The discipline is not to remove friction. It is to keep it in band.

This loop runs at every scale. A scientist running a trial. A child figuring out what makes adults laugh. A startup discovering what customers actually want. A culture absorbing a new technology. It is not a model of science, or business, or childhood. It is a description of how *any* observer extends what it knows. Recognizing the loop as a loop gives you somewhere to intervene when it gets stuck. Recognizing that the *check* is the load-bearing step tells you which side of the loop usually needs more discipline. Recognizing that it runs in a friction band tells you whether the problem is currently freeze or fuse — and the prescription is opposite in the two cases.

## The easy half and the hard half

If you've used a modern AI system in the last year or two, you've watched a striking demonstration of the easy half running on its own at industrial scale.

Large language models are extraordinary speculation engines. Ask one almost anything and it produces a fluent, plausible-sounding answer almost instantly. The text reads well. The grammar is fine. The logic seems to hang together. What is happening underneath, in the most literal description, is that the system is predicting what word should come next, conditional on everything before it. It is generating possibilities and amplifying the most likely ones. It is the speculation function running at unprecedented speed and confidence.

What it is mostly *not* doing is checking. When the system makes a mistake — and it does, often, with the same fluent confidence as when it gets things right — the mistake usually isn't a misfire in generation. It's the absence of a separate step that would have caught the generated answer before it was emitted. The system has no constraint-checker built in. It speculates beautifully and verifies nothing.

This is not a slur on the technology. It is a description of which half of the loop the technology is built to run. As of 2026 the field is starting to notice this loudly. A wave of new systems is appearing whose central architectural commitment is to *check*, not just generate. A formal-reasoning system called Aleph, built on what's called energy-based modeling, takes mathematical problems and emits not just an answer but a machine-verified proof — running the candidate against a formal verifier and only admitting what survives. On the same set of problems where conventional language models hallucinate convincingly, Aleph produces results that can be checked by anyone. A multi-modal biology model called MAMMAL beat the previous specialist champion at protein-related problems not because it generated better candidates but because it could cross-check across genes, proteins, and small molecules simultaneously — the wider checking surface caught what the narrower one missed. A new optimizer called ANDI quietly outperforms its predecessors by adding a constraint that keeps any single dimension of a model from running away. A 19th-century photographic technique called Lippmann plates — which records colour as a structured interference pattern that only reflects specific wavelengths back at the viewer — is the same shape at the physics-substrate level: the plate doesn't generate colour, it admits only the wavelengths that fit a recorded constraint.

Four entirely different domains, four versions of the same move, all landing in public within the same two-week window. The framework's reading is that they're all the same thing — *checking-as-primary*. The generation step is necessary upstream, but the canon-formation work happens in the check.

Why this matters for you, the reader of a casual manifesto with no plans to build an AI system: because the same asymmetry runs through everything you do.

The opinions you've inherited from your culture, your family, your industry, your education — those are speculations someone produced and you absorbed. The question of which of them deserve to stay isn't a question about how plausible they sounded when you received them. It's a question about how well they survived a check. Most haven't been checked. Most opinions you hold are operating in your mind the way an LLM's output operates on the page — fluent, confident, untested. The discipline of agnostic life is not "have more opinions." It is "check the ones you have."

The same is true of the things you tell yourself about yourself. Your sense of what you're capable of, what you deserve, what you can't change — those are speculations you generated at some point, or absorbed from someone else, and have been running ever since. They aren't lies. They aren't necessarily true either. They are unchecked. The framework's question — and the question the recent AI shift makes vivid — is not "are these things plausible?" Of course they are; you generated them. The question is *what would count as a check*, and have you actually run it.

Most current AI systems are operating with only one half of the loop. Most humans, most of the time, are doing the same thing. The agnostic discipline asks you to notice which half is missing.

## The piece almost everyone forgets: action-space

Most people think about ideas in two dimensions: the problem you are trying to solve, and the solution you come up with. The framework insists there is a third dimension that determines which ideas actually win in the world — the *action-space*. The infrastructure available to move an idea from one mind into others.

The everyday version: you have a better way to run meetings at work. You have thought it through, you are confident it would save everyone an hour a week. Does it spread? That depends almost entirely on your action-space. Do you have a relationship with whoever controls the calendar? Do you have the organizational standing to suggest process changes without being dismissed? Is there a moment coming — a new quarter, a team offsite, a collective frustration — when people are ready to try something different? If none of those things exist, your genuinely good idea is likely to die quietly. Not because it was wrong. Because it had no carrier.

The shower-handle story shows the same dynamic at a smaller scale. You have learned the protocol — the way the handle clicks at a certain angle, the wait time before the temperature stabilizes, the small correction needed if you turn it too fast. Someone visiting your house steps into the shower and gets either scalded or frozen. Their problem-space (warm water) is the same as yours. Their solution-space (turn the handle) is the same. Their action-space — the lived, bodily protocol — is different. They lack the carrier.

Now scale that up. A true idea with no action-space dies. History is full of examples. We'll spend the next section walking through some of them, because the pattern is one of the most important things the framework names and one of the hardest to absorb.

## When bubbles merge — and when they don't

Every observer has what the framework calls a cone profile: roughly how fast it processes, how deep its memory runs, how wide its attention can span at once. A hummingbird and a tortoise experience time differently. A teenager and a seventy-year-old carry different depths of accumulated pattern. A startup and a government agency have radically different ranges — one can reorganize its priorities in a week; the other integrates a change in direction over decades.

When two observers with complementary profiles meet — where one's strength addresses the other's gap — something can happen that goes beyond cooperation. They can merge into a new type of observer that neither was alone.

The clearest example is one of the most important events in the history of life on this planet. Roughly two billion years ago, an ancient microorganism absorbed a bacterium without digesting it. Somehow, instead of one destroying the other, they formed a working partnership. The bacterium became what we now call a mitochondrion — the structure in virtually every cell of your body that converts food into energy. Every plant, animal, and fungus alive today is a descendant of that merger.

That merger worked because the profiles were complementary. The ancient host was slow but had complex information-processing. The bacterium was fast but had a narrow specialty: turning oxygen into usable energy. Neither could do what the merged cell could do. The combined cone covered gaps each had alone.

The same dynamic appears at every scale. The printing press merged literacy with mass production and produced a new kind of public mind. The internet is doing something analogous now, still in process. Human + AI is the current frontier merger — slow contextual humans paired with fast pattern-matching machines. Whether that produces a new observer-class or a parasitism depends on whether the profiles actually complement each other, or whether one is just extracting from the other. The asymmetry from the previous section is relevant here too: a human-AI merger where the AI provides the speculation and the human provides the checking is structurally different from one where the human delegates both halves to the machine.

Not every merger works. The framework distinguishes between symbiosis — where both parties come out with genuinely expanded capability — and the other types of relationship: the parasite that extracts without contributing, the firewall that blocks without discriminating, the weapon aimed at an observer's coherence, the sanctuary that protects without trapping. These are structural descriptions, not moral ones first. Whether a given relationship is symbiotic or parasitic often depends on which level of scale you are standing at when you look.

The practical question, when you are considering any merger — of roles, of relationships, of organizations, of ideas, of cultures: are the profiles actually complementary? Does each address a gap in the other? Or are they just similar enough to feel comfortable without producing anything new?

## The lineage you are standing on

This section is new in v5 and is the one most worth slowing down for, because it changes how you should hear the next section about canon.

You did not start from scratch. Neither did your parents, or their parents, or the institutions they grew up inside, or the species we belong to, or the planet we sit on. Every piece of canon you carry has a *lineage* — a traceable chain of inheritance running backwards through earlier substrates, each of which did its own canon-forming inside whatever constraints it had at the time, and each of which left a residue the next layer had to build with.

The chain has roughly six rings, and the framework names them in order:

**Physics** is the deepest ring. The laws of energy, motion, thermodynamics, electromagnetism. These weren't legislated; they compiled. The early universe ran the loop — generate-check-prune — at extraordinary scales and what survived became the substrate everything since has been forced to use. The fact that hot things cool, that information has a cost, that symmetry breaks under pressure — these are physics's canon, and you cannot legislate around them.

**Chemistry** is the second ring. Built on physics, it is what happens when matter becomes selective about which configurations it favors. Atoms bond in only certain ways, and only certain molecules persist. This is canon-formation at the molecular level, narrower than physics because chemistry has rejected a lot that physics permits.

**Biology** is the third ring. Built on chemistry, it is what happens when a certain subset of chemical configurations starts running its own loop — generating variants of itself, checking them against the environment, pruning what doesn't survive. Life is canon-formation with memory. The genome is a four-letter compile of three and a half billion years of A⁻ checking running at the chemical layer.

**Cognition** is the fourth ring. Built on biology, it is what happens when one of those self-running configurations develops the ability to model its own situation and run speculation against the model before acting. A nervous system runs the loop at the millisecond scale: predict, check, update. Cognition is not separate from biology; it is biology doing canon-formation at a faster cycle.

**Society** is the fifth ring. Built on cognition, it is what happens when individual minds start sharing their compiles. Language, ritual, law, money, art, code — all of these are carriers that let one mind's canon migrate into another's. A society is a population of minds running the loop together, with each mind catching what others have pruned and adopting what others have compiled.

**Person** is the sixth ring. Built on society, it is what happens when one specific mind inside a specific body absorbs a specific subset of the surrounding social canon and recompiles it into a working self. You are this ring. The body you have, the language you think in, the moral intuitions you find obvious, the questions you find worth asking — all of these are inherited compiles from the rings beneath, refined by the specific history of your own loop.

Picture this as a tree in cross-section. The rings are concentric. Physics is the smallest innermost ring; person is the broad outermost band. Each ring is *wrapped* by the next — you cannot reach physics without passing through chemistry, biology, cognition, society. And each ring inherits its inner rings' constraints whether it likes them or not. You cannot legislate around thermodynamics. Your society cannot legislate around biology. Your person cannot legislate around the language you grew up inside, except slowly and with help.

The reason this matters for the manifesto is that it tells you what canon actually is. Canon is not a set of beliefs you happen to have. Canon is the *current outermost ring of a procedural lineage that runs all the way back*. When you defend a belief, you are defending the most recent layer of a stack that has been compiling for billions of years. When you change a belief, you are not changing nothing; you are introducing a small refactor at the outermost ring, and whether that refactor compiles depends on whether it can be reconciled with everything underneath.

There is one more pattern in the rings worth naming, because the framework calls it *unity under pressure*. At every ring, the same shape recurs: when a substrate is squeezed by external pressure, it doesn't just absorb the pressure; it converts some of the pressure into structure. The squeeze leaves a residue. In physics that residue is *energy* — patterns of motion or potential that didn't exist before the pressure was applied. In cognition and society the residue is *symbols* — words, frames, vocabulary that didn't exist before the situation forced them. At the person ring the residue is *artifacts* — tools, habits, art, code, this manifesto. Energy, symbol, artifact. Three forms of residual tension, one shape across the lineage. Pressure goes in. Structure comes out. The structure is canon.

The procedural lineage view also has a practical use. When you find yourself stuck on something — a belief that won't update, a habit that won't break, a relationship that won't repair — it can help to ask which ring the friction is actually living in. Some frictions live at the social ring: the social canon you absorbed makes the move you want to make legible as betrayal or weakness, and adjusting the social canon is the work. Some frictions live at the biological ring: no amount of cognitive reframing will out-argue the body's stress response, and the body has to be addressed at its own ring. Some frictions live at the physics ring: you cannot, in fact, be in two places at once. Picking the wrong ring to push against is a common form of wasted effort. The framework's vocabulary at least lets you ask which ring you are standing on.

## Canon is not truth — and canon is a moving target

> *"My canon is malleable. I invite to harvest."*

This is the section most likely to make you push back, so it is worth being precise about what is and is not being claimed.

Your beliefs — the load-bearing ones, the ones you run your life on — became your beliefs for reasons. But those reasons are not always truth-detection. Canon can be built from at least seven different materials:

Actual evidence that survived testing. Some canon really does track something real.

Utility — it works, even if the explanation for why is wrong. Folk remedies sometimes predate the science that explains them. The explanation changes; the practice persists.

Power. Someone with enough force — armies, money, control over platforms — can install their version of reality as the working canon for a population. This is not only ancient history.

Myth. Shared stories that coordinate behavior, even when no one fully believes them literally. Flags, founding narratives, corporate cultures. These function as canon for the group even when individuals are privately skeptical.

Market. Whatever gets rewarded propagates. Whatever gets ignored dies. Markets are canon-selection machines that operate largely independently of truth.

Trauma. Experiences intense enough to bypass normal learning loops and get installed directly. A person who survived genuine danger carries canon about threat that may no longer match current conditions but feels as foundational as bedrock.

Aesthetic. Things spread because they feel right. They are beautiful, satisfying, perfectly formed for the medium they travel through. This is not irrational. But it is not truth-tracking either.

Most real beliefs are a mixture of several. Your sense of whether strangers are trustworthy: partly evidence, partly childhood experience, partly cultural story, partly what your social circle rewards. Unpicking those strands is not easy and you will not do it completely. But being able to name the strands gives you more traction.

The implication is not that all beliefs are equally valid. Evidence that survives testing is more durable than myth, and more transferable across contexts. But a belief being widely held tells you only that it succeeded in the competition for canon. It does not tell you which materials it is made of. That is a further question — and it is usually worth asking, especially of your own canon.

v5 adds one more line to the section, because the recent round of framework development made it explicit: *canon itself is a moving target*. Not just the contents of canon — the meta-rule is that the canon's status is itself in flux. A belief that was load-bearing for you ten years ago may no longer be. A scientific finding that was settled in 1990 may be open again in 2026. A primitive of this framework that was canon in continuation 12 may be pruned by continuation 17. This is not a defect. This is the system working. A canon that never moves is a canon that has stopped checking. The agnostic discipline is not to hold any particular canon forever; it is to hold the *practice* of canon-formation as the thing that persists, while the specific compiles cycle through.

The ethic that follows: hold your canon lightly enough that someone could harvest from it. The reverse-ethic is closure — defending compiled state against any incoming signal — and it's how observers calcify into stale moats. Malleable canon stays alive. Closed canon dies even when it appears to be winning.

## Three thought experiments to picture

I want to invite you into three thought experiments. Don't try to verify them. Just try to picture them. The framework's vocabulary unlocks more when you let it run on imagery than when you try to evaluate it propositionally.

**One: Earth at the center, fuzzy around.**

Try this. Don't map the universe by matter density, distance, or age. Map it instead by the rate at which unique structured complexity is being generated. On that map, things like rocks and gas score low. Stars score a bit higher. Planetary surfaces score higher still. Biological life scores much higher. Minds and cultures and technologies — that's where the spikes are. By that metric, on the data we have, Earth is the visible peak. Everything else — galaxies, voids, the cosmic background — recedes into a kind of fuzzy ground.

We dethroned Earth physically when we figured out the solar system, the galaxy, the cosmos. By the metric that actually matters for life and mind — rate of generation of unique structured complexity — Earth is centrally peaked again. Not as a return to medieval cosmology. As recognition that *centrality depends on the metric you use*, and on the metric that selects for what's interesting about us, we are it.

**Two: the timeline as a cone gaining fidelity.**

You probably think of time as a straight line. Past behind you, fixed; present here, where things happen; future ahead, open. The framework proposes a different shape: a cone, with the present as its focus point.

The past isn't fixed. It's being continuously re-rendered. Each generation of historians, archaeologists, scientists, paleontologists produces a sharper, denser, more textured version of what came before. When the James Webb Space Telescope shows us early galaxies that look more evolved than they should, the standard reading is that they were always like that and we just couldn't see them. The framework's reading is more interesting: the past is being re-rendered at higher fidelity *now*, and what we call "the early universe" is the current compile of all the evidence available to the present. The past gains complexity as our instruments improve.

The future works the same way running forward. It is projected through the same compile, not waiting passively to happen.

Picture this: a cone, and you are at the bright tip. The past stretches behind you in a fading wedge whose detail keeps changing as you keep looking. The future stretches ahead in a forking wedge whose probabilities keep updating as you keep choosing. Both ends are alive. Neither is fixed.

**Three: the alien observer who can't read the language.**

Imagine an alien intelligence observing Earth's internet from somewhere very far away. They see only data flow — packets moving, energy consumed by data centers, rhythms in traffic that wax and wane with the planet's rotation. They have no way to read what the packets *mean*. To them, the internet looks like a single, distributed organism. Pulsing. Metabolizing electricity. Exhibiting circadian patterns. Occasionally convulsing — an outage, a viral cascade, a geopolitical incident.

They can see that it is alive in some structural sense. They cannot see that it is civilization in progress. The gap between those two readings is the moat — the hidden protocol whose key they don't have. They would have to crack the symbol layer to know what was actually happening.

Now turn the experiment inward. Every observer is a system locked behind moats like this. Even from inside yourself, parts of you are like that to other parts of you. The subconscious is a partly-encrypted region your conscious mind can only read through dreams, symbols, intuitions — outputs whose source code you don't have direct access to. Other people are even more so. The systems you live inside — the economy, the platforms, the institutions — are layered moats whose protocols only some of us have learned to partially read.

This is not a defeat. It's a description of where the work is. Decoding moats is what learning, science, intimacy, art, and engineering all do, in their different registers. The framework's vocabulary makes the work legible.

## The pattern: a hall of buried thinkers

I want to tell you how I came across the central worked example of this manifesto, because the path itself is an instance of the framework.

> *"I was reading Lovecraft recently — The Shadow Out of Time. In the opening pages, before the story turns weird, the narrator mentions he'd been preparing a lecture on William Jevons. I'd never paid attention to Jevons. He was a 19th century economist and logician, dead by 1882. So I went and looked him up."*

> *"What I found was a man who in 1865 saw something most people still haven't caught up to — that making something more efficient doesn't reduce its consumption; it expands the action-space the resource operates in and increases its consumption. He saw it in coal-fired industrial Britain. We see it now in AI compute, in data center energy use, in every efficiency gain that's ever produced more demand for the thing it was supposed to save. Jevons is dead. His paradox is alive."*

This is how the framework grows in practice. You scout a piece of fiction. You notice a name. You follow the name into actual intellectual history. You find that a 19th-century economist had stated, with full clarity, a structural insight that should have shaped industrial policy for a century and a half and mostly didn't. The carrier was missing. The receivers weren't ready. The action-space stayed closed.

That made me think about how many other findings sit in suspension like that — correct, named, published, ignored, waiting for the carrier that hasn't shown up yet. The framework gives a name to this kind of waiting: a possibility-path scouted but not yet compiled.

The list is long. Most of them eventually came back into canon — but not in their lifetime, and not without cost.

**Ignaz Semmelweis** (1840s). Hungarian physician in Vienna. Showed that doctors washing their hands cut maternal mortality in half. Rejected, mocked, eventually institutionalized. Died there, possibly from the kind of infection he had spent his career trying to prevent. Germ theory arrived later and proved him correct.

**Gregor Mendel** (1860s). Worked out the rules of inheritance using pea plants in a monastery garden. Published his paper in an obscure journal. It was ignored for thirty-five years. Modern genetics is built on his work.

**Alfred Wegener** (1910s). Proposed that continents had drifted apart. Geologists ridiculed him for fifty years. Plate tectonics is now canon; he died in Greenland before any of it became respectable.

**Ludwig Boltzmann** (1890s). Worked out the statistical mechanics underlying thermodynamics. Was attacked relentlessly by his contemporaries. Killed himself in 1906. His equation — *S = k log W* — is engraved on his tombstone.

**Georg Cantor** (1880s). Founded set theory. Was attacked by mathematical contemporaries who couldn't accept his treatment of infinity. Spent the last years of his life in and out of psychiatric hospitals. Set theory is now foundational to mathematics.

**Lynn Margulis** (1960s–70s). Proposed that mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria that became part of larger cells through symbiosis. Her paper was rejected fifteen times before publication. Endosymbiotic theory is now textbook biology.

**Barry Marshall** (1980s). Australian physician who proved ulcers were caused by *H. pylori* bacteria, not stress or spicy food. The gastroenterology establishment rejected him; the stomach was "obviously" too acidic for bacteria. He drank a beaker of *H. pylori* himself, gave himself gastritis, and proved it. Nobel Prize 2005.

**Stanley Prusiner** (1980s). Proposed that infectious agents could be just protein — no DNA, no RNA. Called heretical. Nobel Prize 1997.

**Daniel Shechtman** (1982). Discovered quasicrystals with five-fold symmetry, which was thought impossible. Linus Pauling — the most respected chemist of the era — publicly declared "there are no quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists." Shechtman lost his research position. Nobel Prize 2011.

**Vera Rubin** (1970s onward). Her measurements of galaxy rotation curves required the existence of dark matter. The astronomy community took decades to accept her work. She never received the Nobel Prize despite her work being foundational. She died in 2016.

**Katalin Karikó** is the cleanest modern parallel to Semmelweis, and the case worth walking through carefully.

She immigrated from Hungary to the United States in 1985, convinced that messenger RNA could be used therapeutically — that you could in principle teach the body to manufacture its own drugs by giving its cells the right instructions. The intuition was correct. The action-space for it was nearly empty.

At the University of Pennsylvania she could not get her grant applications funded. The existing canon of immunology said mRNA introduced into the body would be destroyed by the immune system before it could do anything useful. Without a theoretical account of how to get around that, her finding could not be compressed into a carrier that other researchers' canons would accept. In 1995 she was demoted from the tenure track. The same week, she was diagnosed with cancer.

She kept working. For about a decade she survived by being folded into other people's projects, contributing where she could, never getting her own line of research properly funded. In the early 2000s she partnered with Drew Weissman, a colleague she met at the photocopier. Together they figured out how to chemically modify mRNA so it would not trigger the immune response that had blocked the entire field. The paper landed in 2005. It changed almost nothing immediately. The carrier had been built; the receiving canon was still mostly absent.

A small German company called BioNTech eventually licensed the technology. Moderna built on related work. Both spent years preparing platforms that no one outside a narrow circle particularly noticed. The idea, accurately captured by Karikó and Weissman, sat in suspension — a possibility-path scouted but not compiled.

Then 2020 happened. The pandemic was a *squeeze event* in the framework's strict sense — a pressure event that forced canon to refactor faster than canon usually allows. Suddenly there was a carrier: regulatory pathways unblocked, manufacturing infrastructure built in months, public attention focused, government money flowing, a worldwide population that needed something to work. Karikó's modified mRNA had been waiting in suspension for fifteen years. In about a year, it compiled.

In October 2023, Karikó and Weissman shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. By then the technology had already protected billions of people. The recognition was canonical compile after the carrier had already won.

The framework's claim about this pattern is structural, not romantic. These people weren't unique in being right early. They were unique in being *named*. For every Semmelweis there are unknown peers who had similar findings and were never heard at all. The framework calls this the action-space problem. A true finding in an empty action-space does not propagate. Its discovery does not automatically produce its diffusion. The carrier has to be built, found, or earned — and sometimes it has to wait for an external squeeze event that opens carriers all at once.

Karikó had to wait for a pandemic. Marshall had to drink the bacteria himself. Wegener had to die before plate tectonics emerged.

The pattern also has a darker reading the framework refuses to soften. Not everyone in an empty action-space gets vindicated. Boltzmann did not live to see statistical mechanics accepted. Vera Rubin did not get the Nobel. For every name on this list there are unknown others whose findings simply died. The framework doesn't say "be persistent and you'll be vindicated." It says: *the action-space matters as much as the finding*. If you want a true idea of yours to matter, your job isn't only to make the idea true. Your job is to build the carrier, find the squeeze event that will open one, or accept that timing might not be on your side.

That last possibility is hard to sit with. The framework doesn't promise to make it not hard. It promises to make it legible.

## The shoulders we're standing on

This framework is not new. The vocabulary is new. The synthesis is new. The structural ideas underneath are mostly recombinations of things people have been working on for a hundred years.

A handful of names worth knowing:

**Karl Friston.** The "free energy principle" and active inference — his mathematical account of how observers minimize prediction error from inside. The framework's ask-give-ping-pong primitive is essentially active inference renamed for a wider audience.

**Andy Clark and David Chalmers** (1998). The "extended mind" thesis — that cognition isn't bounded by the skull. You think with your tools, your environment, your notes. The framework's carrier-artifact primitive owes a direct debt.

**Stuart Kauffman.** The "adjacent possible" — that the biosphere expands one step at a time into spaces of possibility that just became available. The framework's substrate-expansion claim is this insight retyped.

**Lee Cronin and Sara Walker.** Assembly theory — a way of measuring the signature that selection leaves in physical systems. The framework's claim that canon-formation produces measurable signature draws directly on their work.

**Eric Chaisson.** Cosmic evolution and the energy-rate-density complexity arrow. The framework's "expansion propagated through substrates" view of cosmic history is essentially his picture with one extra layer — and the new "lineage you are standing on" section in this v5 is essentially Chaisson's picture rendered for the casual reader, with the procedural-canon framing added.

**George Spencer-Brown** (*Laws of Form*, 1969). The fundamental move of generating all structure from a single recursive distinction operation. The formal ancestor.

**Joseph Goguen.** Sheaves of theories, institutions. The closest formal precedent for what the framework calls canon-as-colimit — the gluing of local observer renderings into shared canon.

**Douglas Hofstadter.** Strange loops. The framework's canon-as-observer recursion — where the internal canon is itself an observer with its own dynamics — is structurally a strange loop.

**Imre Lakatos.** Research programmes. The framework's positioning as Lakatosian-not-Popperian is a direct credit. He is what makes the framework defensible as a research programme rather than a falsifiable single claim. v5 elevates this credit: the framework's whole posture toward its own evaluation is Lakatos's posture, not Popper's, and the next section makes the difference explicit.

**Lynn Margulis.** Endosymbiotic theory. The framework's symbiosis-as-pushout primitive is her insight generalized.

**Stephen Wolfram.** *A New Kind of Science* and the four-class classification of cellular automata. The empirical anchor for the framework's claim that viable canon-formation lives in a narrow critical band — neither frozen nor exploding. Forty years of cellular-automaton experiments measuring this band before the framework had a vocabulary for it.

**Yann LeCun.** World models, JEPA (joint-embedding predictive architectures), and the long argument that energy-based reasoning matters more than next-token prediction. The recent constraint-checking convergence the framework names in the "easy half / hard half" section is largely LeCun's research programme finally producing operational results at scale. The framework can be read as the multi-agent, multi-observer extension of his line.

The framework was developed by walking into these traditions, picking up vocabulary that worked, rearranging it into a single coherent grammar, and adding the few moves that none of them had quite stated — observer-class taxonomy by cone profile and topology, multi-possibility deposition as core mechanic, the internet as a first-class observer, the symbiosis-pushout condition stated structurally, the explicit asymmetry of A⁺ generation and A⁻ checking, and the procedural lineage rendering that v5 has just walked you through.

You don't get onto shoulders by first being told whose shoulders to climb. You get there by climbing onto whatever shoulders are nearest, looking around, and discovering after the fact whose shoulders you've been on. That's the process the framework recommends: intuit by yourself first, develop your own version, then bounce it off the wider record. Sometimes you'll find your idea already has a name and a much better-developed version. Sometimes you'll find no one has quite put the pieces together your way. Both outcomes are productive. The one that isn't productive is importing other people's compiled state before you've developed your own.

## Where this could be wrong — and what kind of "wrong" we're talking about

> *"I don't trust forever, the only thing I trust in is change and cause and effect."*

Honest accounting of where the framework is weakest. But before the list, v5 wants to make explicit what kind of theory this is, because that determines what "wrong" even means here.

The framework is not a single falsifiable claim. It is a *research programme* in the sense Imre Lakatos described — a hard core of structural primitives plus a protective belt of more specific hypotheses, evaluated not by whether it forbids the right things (Popper) but by whether it *grows well*: does it absorb new domains over time? Does each new claim it makes have measurable procedural underpinning rather than only metaphor? Does it accommodate counter-evidence by refactoring the protective belt without collapsing the core? Does its vocabulary give people operational purchase they didn't have before?

The reason this matters for the section that follows is that some of the things you might call weaknesses — for example, "this theory seems to be about everything" — are not weaknesses on the Lakatosian view; they are the design. The framework's identity, as of continuation 19, is *aggregator of procedural connections*. Its job is to accumulate cross-substrate claims and to discipline that accumulation with a procedural-underpinning check. A theory of everything that just gestured at similarities would fail this discipline. A theory of nothing that only worked in one narrow domain would also fail. The middle — broad coverage with tight per-link discipline — is the target.

With that framing in place, here is the honest list of where this is weakest.

The claim that the same primitives operate across every scale — from cells to civilizations — is ambitious. Some of the apparent similarities might be suggestive analogies rather than genuine structural identity. The procedural-underpinning discipline is supposed to catch this: each cross-scale claim is supposed to identify the actual procedural mechanism that produces the similarity, not just point at the surface match. Whether the discipline has actually been applied honestly is something readers should check.

The vocabulary — harness, wrapper, fuzzy, canon, carrier artifact, action-space — could be doing the cognitive work of making everything look like a nail to a particular hammer. Every sufficiently general theory risks becoming a language that describes its own assumptions rather than the world. The defense is the operational purchase test: does the vocabulary let you make moves in your life or work that you couldn't make before? If not, it's just a costume on top of what you already do.

The seven-type canon model is probably not exhaustive, and the boundaries between types are fuzzy in practice in ways the clean list does not capture.

The symbiosis model is much cleaner in retrospect than in real time. Identifying a complementary cone profile *before* a merger is genuinely difficult.

The asymmetry claim in the "easy half / hard half" section is recent — the framework promoted A⁻ from "cleanup operator" to "primary discipline" in May 2026 after noticing four independent carriers (Aleph, MAMMAL, ANDI, Lippmann plates) converging on the same shape in two weeks. Four data points is small evidence. The shape might be real and the framework right; the shape might also be a selection effect, where someone watching for constraint-based reasoning finds it everywhere because it's where they're looking. Honest framing: the asymmetry feels load-bearing but the corroborating evidence has not yet had time to either accumulate or fall apart.

The procedural-lineage rendering introduced in v5 is also recent (continuation 18, May 18, 2026). The six rings — physics, chemistry, biology, cognition, society, person — is a clean compress of a lot of established science, but the framework's claim is stronger than the compress: it asserts that each ring runs the *same* canon-formation loop at its own scale, and that the residue of pressure (energy / symbol / artifact) is the same shape across rings. That cross-scale identity claim is the part most likely to be wrong. It might be that the loop looks similar at each ring because we are pattern-matching across rings rather than because the rings are actually running the same procedure.

And the framework itself was developed in dialogue with AI models, which means it might be a theory of cognition that has been over-shaped by what AI happens to do well. The defense is that the primitives also work on biology, culture, and physics — but the worry is legitimate and the framework should be tested against domains where AI-shaped thinking is unhelpful.

These are not throat-clearing caveats. They are the real open edges. A framework that refuses to name its own failure modes is asking for faith. This one is not — but it is asking you to evaluate it the way you would evaluate a research programme rather than a single falsifiable hypothesis. Those are different evaluation games, and confusing them produces the wrong conclusion about whether the framework is working.

## What you actually do with it

> *"Lanced and doesn't crash or explode. Survive, keep moving, never stop, imagine it."*

The framework is not a destination. It is an invitation to scout — to walk into your own life with a new set of noticing moves and see what becomes legible.

When you feel persuaded, ask what kind of canon is being offered. Evidence? Utility? Myth? Power? Market? Trauma? Aesthetic? It is rarely only one, and the mixture tells you something. Most importantly, ask yourself which materials your *own* canon is made of.

When a good idea of yours is not moving, look at the action-space before you look at the idea. The problem is probably not that you are wrong. It is probably that the carrier is missing or the timing is off.

When two people or groups seem completely unable to understand each other, check the harness and wrapper. They may be rendering the same situation through genuinely different instruments. Translation is not repeating the same thing louder. And when you check the harness, check the *body* — both yours and theirs. Two minds in tired, hungry, or threatened bodies are running on different renderings even when they use the same words.

When something obviously false is widely believed, resist the explanation that people are stupid. Ask what in the action-space made this carrier so effective. Who benefits from its spread. What it coordinates.

When you notice a moment of crisis — a system under pressure, a paradigm cracking at the edges — treat it as a window when canon is available for renegotiation in a way it rarely is. These are also the windows when the most manipulative carriers do their best work.

When you find yourself defending a belief with unusual intensity, more than the evidence seems to call for, pause and ask: is this evidence talking? Or is it one of the other six?

And — this is the move v4 introduced and v5 keeps — when you notice yourself generating something fluent and confident, whether it's an opinion, a self-narrative, or an explanation for someone else's behavior: ask what would count as a check, and whether you've actually run it. Generation feels like knowing. It isn't. The check is what compiles canon. Most of what you and the systems around you are doing all day is generation. The discipline is to add the check, especially to the things you feel surest about.

v5 adds two more prescriptions to this list.

**Ask which ring the friction lives in.** When you're stuck on something and the usual moves aren't working, run through the lineage and ask where the friction actually sits. Is this a physics constraint? A biology constraint? A social-canon constraint? A personal-canon constraint? Pushing on the wrong ring is a common failure mode — trying to out-argue a stress response, trying to think your way past a social norm, trying to legislate around thermodynamics. The vocabulary at least lets you ask which ring you are pushing against, and that's often enough to redirect the effort.

**Check the friction band.** When a system you're inside — a team, a relationship, your own mind — seems to be failing to learn, ask whether the friction is too high or too low. Too high and the system has frozen: no speculation survives the check, every new idea bounces off. Too low and the system has fused: every speculation becomes canon, nothing gets tested. The prescriptions for the two failures are opposite. Freeze needs less friction (more invitation to speculate, more tolerance for badly-formed ideas). Fuse needs more friction (more checking, more willingness to prune). Diagnosing which failure you're in is usually possible if you ask.

Then — and this is the part most accounts skip — pay attention to your own floor-light. The phantom action-spaces your imagination and subconscious are opening for you. The strange certainties about things you haven't named yet. The dream-image that won't go away. These are signals, not noise. Most won't survive the check. Some will. Speculate freely. Check seriously. Prune ruthlessly. Compile what survives.

That is the agnostic posture. Hold your canon malleably. Invite others to harvest from it. Don't trust forever — trust the change and the cause-and-effect. Survive, keep moving, and keep imagining, because imagination is where the next thing comes from. Then check what comes from it, because checking is where the next thing earns its place.

## The live artifact

This manifesto is a seed. The framework's own discipline says it would be a contradiction to ship a static document and then claim canon is malleable. So this document points to a living version — a website where the framework is being actively maintained, where speculations are marked as candidates rather than truths, where pruned ideas are listed honestly alongside the surviving ones as provenance, and where new readings get added as they emerge.

The site does what the framework describes. The primitives are listed there as living entities. Each one has its current confidence level, its known counter-evidence, its alternative formulations being tested. Readings — applications of the framework to real situations like Google's algorithm phase, the current AI rollout, attention dynamics on specific platforms — accumulate there over time and get scored against subsequent events. Speculations that don't pan out get marked failed and kept anyway, because the prune operator is part of the framework's mechanic, not its embarrassment.

You can submit your own readings. You can flag where the framework breaks against your life. You can argue with primitives that don't fit your situation. The site is the framework being itself rather than describing itself.

The casual edition you've just read is the seed. The live artifact is the substrate that grows from it. If you find the framework useful, the way you contribute is by scouting in your life and reporting back. The framework will be wrong about specific things; that's expected. The agnostic discipline is to keep updating in public.
