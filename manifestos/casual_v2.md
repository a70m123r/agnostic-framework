# A New Pair of Eyes

## Notes on becoming a fellow scout

**Agnostic manifesto, v2 — casual reader edition**
**Status:** draft for review. Provenance-anchored revision of v1. Pav's voice preserved in quotes; framework synthesis builds around them. Critic notes at end describe what changed from v1.

## A small piece of magic

> *"There's another bit of magic that I have. I can get fire from a lighter when no one else can. At first there was a trick to it — to cup a palm over the lighter and collect the gas — but after a while, after people have tried and given up, I can just get a light without the ritual. To them it looks like magic. To me it's a hidden protocol I learned to read."*

Almost everyone has one of these. A trick that works in your hands and looks like magic in someone else's. The shower whose handle you've learned to nudge in just the right way. The car door that opens if you lift while you turn. The colleague who only stops talking if you ask exactly the right question. To you these are not mysterious. To anyone watching, they look like access to something they can't quite see.

The Agnostic framework starts there. Not with grand theory. With the recognition that hidden protocols are everywhere, that some of them you have learned to read and most of them you haven't, and that the world you live in is shaped much more by these invisible operating procedures than by anything written down.

This is a thinking tool. It will not tell you what is true. It will give you a vocabulary for noticing how things become true — how ideas travel, stick, mutate, and win; why some true things vanish without a trace and some false things reshape civilizations; how you are, right now, the compressed output of more influences than you can count. And how to participate in that process more deliberately than you currently do.

I am not asking you to believe the framework. I am inviting you to scout it — to walk into the speculation it spawns and see what holds up in your own life.

## You are a renderer. You are also a receiver.

The first move the framework makes is the strangest one, so it is worth going slowly.

We usually treat the world as something that arrives at us — information comes in, we absorb it, we know things. But consider what actually happens when you see a face across a room and immediately feel something — like or dislike, trust or wariness. You did not consult evidence. You ran a fast pattern-match against everything you have ever experienced involving faces, voices, posture, context. The result felt like direct perception. It was not. It was a rendering.

That much is what most of cognitive science already says. The Agnostic move is to add the symmetry that most accounts skip:

> *"I have a swarm of inner selves... they get conjured up in the sandbox, however they are always active in the subconscious."*

You are not only rendering signals from outside. You are also *receiving* signals from inside. From the subconscious. From imagination. From dreams. From an intuition that arrives before the conscious knowing of it. These are not noise. They are real signals, traveling up into awareness from underneath, opening what the framework calls *phantom action-spaces* — possibility-paths that haven't been tested yet but feel weighty enough to attend to.

Most accounts of the mind treat conscious experience as the result of two operations: information coming in, and processing producing experience. The framework's claim is that there are two flows, not one. Outside signals come in. Inside signals come up. The conscious mind is where they meet and get sorted into what feels like reality.

Imagine, for a moment, your awareness as a small bright room. Light enters from one wall — that's the outside flow: senses, conversations, news, weather, other people. Light also rises from the floor — that's the inside flow: hunches, dreams, half-formed thoughts, the strange certainty about something you haven't seen evidence for yet. You are standing in the room. Both lights are real. Both are partial. Together they produce what you experience as a complete picture.

The thing nobody told you, and the thing this framework insists on, is that the floor-light is doing as much work as the wall-light. And learning to read the floor-light is the beginning of becoming a scout for yourself.

## Speculation is the engine

> *"Speculation is part of the process and selective barrier of how new kernel canon is created based on legacy. A phantom action space opens and then that gets mapped on legacy and evaluated as the next block or brick."*

Here is the move that most accounts of thinking miss entirely.

Speculation — the deliberate generation of possibilities you can't yet verify — is not a bug to be managed. It is the standard mechanism by which what you know extends itself. Every piece of canon you carry was once a speculation. Germ theory. The idea that another person can be trusted. The notion that you could be loved. The certainty that water boils at a particular temperature. All of these started as phantom action-spaces opened by subconscious or imagination, then mapped against your existing canon to see if they fit, then either accepted, rejected, or held in suspense.

The framework distinguishes three things people often confuse:

**Imagination** — the floor-light. Phantom action-spaces opening up. Possibilities you can hold in mind without committing to them. Children do this constantly. Adults often forget how.

**Hypothesis** — a speculation that has been named, made articulate, and proposed for testing. "I wonder if this is the case. Let me see what would count as evidence."

**Insight** — a speculation that survived feedback. It changed your map. You now run your life slightly differently because of it.

**Delusion** — a speculation that hardened into canon *before* feedback. The phantom action-space was treated as already-real before it earned that status.

The whole game is in the middle. Speculate freely. Test seriously. Prune ruthlessly. Accept what survives. If you suppress the speculation step entirely, you cut off the canon-extension mechanism and stop learning. If you let speculation run without testing, you slide into delusion. Neither extreme is stable. The framework names the discipline that operates between them.

The reason this matters for the manifesto is that I am asking you, right now, to do the imagination step. To open phantom action-spaces around the ideas that follow. Not to believe them. To picture them. To see what holds in your own life when you turn the framework's vocabulary loose on situations you actually inhabit.

That is the agnostic purpose. Not to install a worldview. To spark the speculation function.

## The loop everything runs on

At any given moment, every observer — you, your friend, your employer, your country, the algorithm sorting your feed — is sitting somewhere between fuzzy and canon.

Fuzzy is the open territory. The things you have noticed but not yet integrated. The questions without answers. The situations where multiple interpretations are still on the table. Right now, some things in your mind are fuzzy: a relationship you have not yet figured out, a news story you only half-understood, a feeling you cannot quite name. Fuzzy is not a problem to be solved. It is the live state, the searching state, the state where learning is possible.

Canon is what has compiled. The things that survived enough testing that you are running them as operational assumptions. Your native language is canon. The floor being solid is canon. Your sense of whether you are a person people trust is canon. Canon does not mean eternal. It means load-bearing right now. The thing you are standing on while you reach for the next thing.

The loop that connects them is the framework's central engine. Something fuzzy catches attention — a gap, an anomaly, a nagging question. You reach: you ask someone, read something, try an experiment. Something comes back. If what comes back survives your coherence check, it starts to compress into something portable: a word, a habit, a rule of thumb, a tool. The framework calls this a *carrier artifact*. It is now doing work for you without requiring constant attention. Enough carrier artifacts on the same subject eventually crystallize into a chunk of your operating canon.

> *"The give could be a new substrate aggregate compile when novel insight is gained."*

This loop runs at every scale. A scientist running a trial. A child figuring out what makes adults laugh. A startup discovering what customers actually want. A culture absorbing a new technology. It is not a model of science, or business, or childhood. It is a description of how *any* observer extends what it knows.

Recognizing the loop as a loop gives you somewhere to intervene when it gets stuck.

## The piece almost everyone forgets: action-space

Most people think about ideas in two dimensions: the problem you are trying to solve, and the solution you come up with. The framework insists there is a third dimension that determines which ideas actually win in the world — the *action-space*. The infrastructure available to move an idea from one mind into others.

The everyday version: you have a better way to run meetings at work. You have thought it through, you are confident it would save everyone an hour a week. Does it spread? That depends almost entirely on your action-space. Do you have a relationship with whoever controls the calendar? Do you have the organizational standing to suggest process changes without being dismissed? Is there a moment coming — a new quarter, a team offsite, a collective frustration — when people are ready to try something different? If none of those things exist, your genuinely good idea is likely to die quietly. Not because it was wrong. Because it had no carrier.

The shower-handle story shows the same dynamic at a smaller scale. You have learned the protocol — the way the handle clicks at a certain angle, the wait time before the temperature stabilizes, the small correction needed if you turn it too fast. Someone visiting your house steps into the shower and gets either scalded or frozen. Their problem-space (warm water) is the same as yours. Their solution-space (turn the handle) is the same. Their action-space — the lived, bodily protocol — is different. They lack the carrier.

Now scale that up. A true idea with no action-space dies. History is full of examples: ideas that were correct, that someone had clearly and early, that vanished because the person who had them had no platform, no theory that made the idea legible to the existing canon, no institutional position, no moment. And history is equally full of ideas that were wrong, or harmful, or simply mediocre, that spread everywhere — because they had infrastructure, timing, the right form for the medium they were moving through, the backing of someone with force.

This is not cynicism. It is a design insight. If you have a true thing and you want it to matter, your job is not only to make the thing true. Your job is to build the carrier. The medium matters. The timing matters. Whether the idea comes as a story, a spreadsheet, a slogan, a ritual, a song — it matters enormously.

## When bubbles merge — and when they don't

Every observer has what the framework calls a cone profile: roughly how fast it processes, how deep its memory runs, how wide its attention can span at once. A hummingbird and a tortoise experience time differently. A teenager and a seventy-year-old carry different depths of accumulated pattern. A startup and a government agency have radically different ranges — one can reorganize its priorities in a week; the other integrates a change in direction over decades.

When two observers with complementary profiles meet — where one's strength addresses the other's gap — something can happen that goes beyond cooperation. They can merge into a new type of observer that neither was alone.

The clearest example is one of the most important events in the history of life on this planet. Roughly two billion years ago, an ancient microorganism absorbed a bacterium without digesting it. Somehow, instead of one destroying the other, they formed a working partnership. The bacterium became what we now call a mitochondrion — the structure in virtually every cell of your body that converts food into energy. Every plant, animal, and fungus alive today is a descendant of that merger.

That merger worked because the profiles were complementary. The ancient host was slow but had complex information-processing. The bacterium was fast but had a narrow specialty: turning oxygen into usable energy. Neither could do what the merged cell could do. The combined cone covered gaps each had alone.

The same dynamic appears at every scale. The printing press merged literacy with mass production and produced a new kind of public mind — a literate public capable of shared debate across geography. The internet is doing something analogous now, still in process, still terrifying and extraordinary in ways we cannot yet fully describe. Human + AI is the current frontier merger — slow contextual humans paired with fast pattern-matching machines. Whether that produces a new observer-class or a parasitism depends on whether the profiles actually complement each other, or whether one is just extracting from the other.

Not every merger works. The framework distinguishes between symbiosis — where both parties come out with genuinely expanded capability — and the other types of relationship: the parasite that extracts without contributing, the firewall that blocks without discriminating, the weapon aimed at an observer's coherence, the sanctuary that protects without trapping. These are not moral categories first. They are structural descriptions. Whether a given relationship is symbiotic or parasitic often depends on which level of scale you are standing at when you look.

The practical question, when you are considering any merger — of roles, of relationships, of organizations, of ideas, of cultures: are the profiles actually complementary? Does each address a gap in the other? Or are they just similar enough to feel comfortable without producing anything new?

## Canon is not truth

> *"My canon is malleable. I invite to harvest."*

This is the section most likely to make you push back, so it is worth being precise about what is and is not being claimed.

Your beliefs — the load-bearing ones, the ones you run your life on — became your beliefs for reasons. But those reasons are not always truth-detection. Canon can be built from at least seven different materials:

Actual evidence that survived testing. Some canon really does track something real.

Utility — it works, even if the explanation for why is wrong. Folk remedies sometimes predate the science that explains them. The explanation changes; the practice persists.

Power. Someone with enough force — armies, money, control over platforms — can install their version of reality as the working canon for a population. This is not only ancient history.

Myth. Shared stories that coordinate behavior, even when no one fully believes them literally. Flags, founding narratives, corporate cultures. These function as canon for the group even when individuals are privately skeptical.

Market. Whatever gets rewarded propagates. Whatever gets ignored dies. Markets are canon-selection machines that operate largely independently of truth.

Trauma. Experiences intense enough to bypass normal learning loops and get installed directly. A person who survived genuine danger carries canon about threat that may no longer match current conditions but feels as foundational as bedrock.

Aesthetic. Things spread because they feel right. They are beautiful, satisfying, perfectly formed for the medium they travel through. This is not irrational. But it is not truth-tracking either.

Most real beliefs are a mixture of several. Your sense of whether strangers are trustworthy: partly evidence, partly childhood experience, partly cultural story, partly what your social circle rewards. Unpicking those strands is not easy and you will not do it completely. But being able to name the strands gives you more traction.

The implication is not that all beliefs are equally valid. Evidence that survives testing is more durable than myth, and more transferable across contexts. But a belief being widely held tells you only that it succeeded in the competition for canon. It does not tell you which materials it is made of. That is a further question — and it is usually worth asking, especially of your own canon.

The ethic that follows: hold your canon lightly enough that someone could harvest from it. The reverse-ethic is closure — defending compiled state against any incoming signal — and it's how observers calcify into stale moats. Malleable canon stays alive. Closed canon dies even when it appears to be winning.

## Three thought experiments to picture

I want to invite you into three thought experiments. Don't try to verify them. Just try to picture them. The framework's vocabulary unlocks more when you let it run on imagery than when you try to evaluate it propositionally.

**One: Earth at the center, fuzzy around.**

Try this. Don't map the universe by matter density, distance, or age. Map it instead by the rate at which unique structured complexity is being generated. On that map, things like rocks and gas score low. Stars score a bit higher. Planetary surfaces score higher still. Biological life scores much higher. Minds and cultures and technologies — that's where the spikes are. By that metric, on the data we have, Earth is the visible peak. Everything else — galaxies, voids, the cosmic background — recedes into a kind of fuzzy ground.

We dethroned Earth physically when we figured out the solar system, the galaxy, the cosmos. By the metric that actually matters for life and mind — rate of generation of unique structured complexity — Earth is centrally peaked again. Not as a return to medieval cosmology. As recognition that *centrality depends on the metric you use*, and on the metric that selects for what's interesting about us, we are it.

This is one of several ways to remap reality through the framework's vocabulary. Try it as a posture for a day: look at the world as if everything you see is either a spike in the complexity-generation field or part of the fuzzy ground around it. Notice what becomes legible.

**Two: the timeline as a cone gaining fidelity.**

You probably think of time as a straight line. Past behind you, fixed; present here, where things happen; future ahead, open. The framework proposes a different shape: a cone, with the present as its focus point.

The past isn't fixed. It's being continuously re-rendered. Each generation of historians, archaeologists, scientists, paleontologists produces a sharper, denser, more textured version of what came before. When the James Webb Space Telescope shows us early galaxies that look more evolved than they should, the standard reading is that they were always like that and we just couldn't see them. The framework's reading is more interesting: the past is being re-rendered at higher fidelity *now*, and what we call "the early universe" is the current compile of all the evidence available to the present. The past gains complexity as our instruments improve.

The future works the same way running forward. It is projected through the same compile, not waiting passively to happen. What you imagine the next ten years to be is shaping what those years will become, because your imagination is part of the action-space those years draw from.

Picture this: a cone, and you are at the bright tip. The past stretches behind you in a fading wedge whose detail keeps changing as you keep looking. The future stretches ahead in a forking wedge whose probabilities keep updating as you keep choosing. Both ends are alive. Neither is fixed.

**Three: the alien observer who can't read the language.**

Imagine an alien intelligence observing Earth's internet from somewhere very far away. They see only data flow — packets moving, energy consumed by data centers, rhythms in traffic that wax and wane with the planet's rotation. They have no way to read what the packets *mean*. To them, the internet looks like a single, distributed organism. Pulsing. Metabolizing electricity. Exhibiting circadian patterns. Occasionally convulsing — an outage, a viral cascade, a geopolitical incident.

They can see that it is alive in some structural sense. They cannot see that it is civilization in progress. The gap between those two readings is the moat — the hidden protocol whose key they don't have. They would have to crack the symbol layer to know what was actually happening.

Now turn the experiment inward. Every observer is a system locked behind moats like this. Even from inside yourself, parts of you are like that to other parts of you. The subconscious is a partly-encrypted region your conscious mind can only read through dreams, symbols, intuitions — outputs whose source code you don't have direct access to. Other people are even more so. The systems you live inside — the economy, the platforms, the institutions — are layered moats whose protocols only some of us have learned to partially read.

This is not a defeat. It's a description of where the work is. Decoding moats is what learning, science, intimacy, art, and engineering all do, in their different registers. The framework's vocabulary makes the work legible.

## Where this could be wrong

> *"I don't trust forever, the only thing I trust in is change and cause and effect."*

Honest accounting of where the framework is weakest.

The claim that the same primitives operate across every scale — from cells to civilizations — is ambitious. The similarities between biological symbiosis and cultural merger might be suggestive analogies rather than genuine structural identity. Scale might introduce discontinuities that make cross-scale reasoning misleading in ways that are not yet apparent.

The vocabulary — harness, wrapper, fuzzy, canon, carrier artifact, action-space — could be doing the cognitive work of making everything look like a nail to a particular hammer. Every sufficiently general theory risks becoming a language that describes its own assumptions rather than the world.

The seven-type canon model is probably not exhaustive, and the boundaries between types are fuzzy in practice in ways the clean list does not capture.

The symbiosis model is much cleaner in retrospect than in real time. Identifying a complementary cone profile *before* a merger is genuinely difficult.

And the framework itself was developed in dialogue with AI models, which means it might be a theory of cognition that has been over-shaped by what AI happens to do well. The defense is that the primitives also work on biology, culture, and physics — but the worry is legitimate and the framework should be tested against domains where AI-shaped thinking is unhelpful.

These are not throat-clearing caveats. They are the real open edges. A framework that refuses to name its own failure modes is asking for faith. This one is not.

## What you actually do with it

> *"Lanced and doesn't crash or explode. Survive, keep moving, never stop, imagine it."*

The framework is not a destination. It is an invitation to scout — to walk into your own life with a new set of noticing moves and see what becomes legible.

When you feel persuaded, ask what kind of canon is being offered. Evidence? Utility? Myth? Power? Market? Trauma? Aesthetic? It is rarely only one, and the mixture tells you something. Most importantly, ask yourself which materials your *own* canon is made of.

When a good idea of yours is not moving, look at the action-space before you look at the idea. The problem is probably not that you are wrong. It is probably that the carrier is missing or the timing is off.

When two people or groups seem completely unable to understand each other, check the harness and wrapper. They may be rendering the same situation through genuinely different instruments. Translation is not repeating the same thing louder.

When something obviously false is widely believed, resist the explanation that people are stupid. Ask what in the action-space made this carrier so effective. Who benefits from its spread. What it coordinates.

When you notice a moment of crisis — a system under pressure, a paradigm cracking at the edges — treat it as a window when canon is available for renegotiation in a way it rarely is. These are also the windows when the most manipulative carriers do their best work.

And when you find yourself defending a belief with unusual intensity, more than the evidence seems to call for, pause and ask: is this evidence talking? Or is it one of the other six?

Then — and this is the part most accounts skip — pay attention to your own floor-light. The phantom action-spaces your imagination and subconscious are opening for you. The strange certainties about things you haven't named yet. The dream-image that won't go away. These are signals, not noise. Most won't survive testing. Some will. Speculate freely. Test seriously. Prune ruthlessly. Compile what survives.

That is the agnostic posture. Hold your canon malleably. Invite others to harvest from it. Don't trust forever — trust the change and the cause-and-effect. Survive, keep moving, and keep imagining, because imagination is where the next thing comes from.

You do not have to believe any of this. The invitation is only to scout. If the vocabulary gives you somewhere new to stand while you're looking, keep it. If it doesn't, leave it. The framework's own discipline says it has to earn its keep in your life or get pruned.

That's the deal.

## Notes on what this revision changed from v1

The v1 draft (by a sonnet writer agent) was polished and accessible but missed three structural things Pav flagged:

**The symmetry move.** The v1 described the reader as a renderer of outside signals. It didn't describe the reader as also a *receiver* of inside signals — from subconscious, imagination, dreams, intuition. The v2 makes this symmetry explicit. The "floor-light" framing is introduced in §2 and runs through the closing. Inside signals open phantom action-spaces; that's what gives speculation its raw material.

**The function of speculation.** The v1 mentioned speculation once. The v2 makes it a dedicated section (§3) with the four-way distinction: imagination, hypothesis, insight, delusion. The framework's claim — *speculation is the engine of canon extension, not the exception* — is the manifesto's central operational insight and the v1 buried it. The v2 leads with it after the substrate frame.

**Visual thought experiments.** The v1 was prose-only. The v2 adds three pictureable thought experiments in §8: Earth-at-the-center under a complexity-generation metric; the timeline as a cone whose past gains fidelity as the compile sharpens; the alien observer who sees the internet's metabolism but not its meaning. These invite the reader's imagination rather than just transmitting information.

**Provenance format.** The v1 was synthesized in a third-person framework voice. The v2 alternates between Pav's verbatim quotes (preserved with original typos and rhythm) and framework synthesis built around them. The personal voice is the anchor; the framework is the scaffolding. This is closer to Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* in structure than to a polished academic essay — personal notes whose framework emerges from lived material.

**The lighter trick as opening.** Replaced v1's "moment the pull becomes visible" hook with Pav's actual lighter quote. The lighter trick is a perfect framework instance: hidden protocol → compressed skill → looks like magic from outside. Plus it carries the personal voice from the first line.

**The closing.** The v1 ended on "somewhere new to stand while you are looking." The v2 ends with Pav's *"Lanced and doesn't crash or explode. Survive, keep moving, never stop, imagine it"* as the discipline statement, then closes with the agnostic posture as invitation. The reader is asked to scout, not to convert.

**The reader-as-fellow-scout posture.** Threaded throughout. The v1's stance was "here is a thinking tool you can use." The v2's stance is "you are already doing some of this; the framework names what you're doing and invites you to do more of it deliberately." Subtle but it changes how every section lands.

Length: about 4,000 words, similar to v1. Same structural skeleton but with the symmetry, the speculation function, the visual thought experiments, and the provenance anchoring as additions.

The v1 file is preserved at `AGNOSTIC_manifesto_v1_casual.md`. This v2 is the recommended starting point for the casual edition.
